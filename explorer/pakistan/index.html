About Pakistan

Pakistan is a large country in South Asia, stretching from the Arabian Sea in the south to the high mountains of the Hindu Kush and Karakoram in the north. It borders India, Afghanistan, Iran and China.

The country is home to a remarkable mixture of languages, ethnic groups, landscapes and cultures. Islamabad is the capital, while Karachi is the largest city and a major commercial port.

The flag of Pakistan

Pakistan's flag has a large dark green field with a vertical white stripe at the hoist. A white crescent and five-pointed star appear on the green field.

The flag was adopted on 11 August 1947, shortly before Pakistan became independent on 14 August. Its design was based on the flag of the All-India Muslim League.

The green field represents the Muslim majority, while the white stripe represents religious minorities. The crescent and star are traditional Islamic symbols.

The green field

The large green field represents the Muslim majority of Pakistan and is closely associated with Islam.

Green has long been an important colour in Islamic tradition and became a major part of the visual identity of the Pakistan Movement.

The white stripe

The vertical white stripe at the hoist represents Pakistan's religious minorities.

Its position beside the green field is intended to show the place of minorities within the national community.

The crescent

The white crescent is a traditional symbol associated with Islam and is also commonly interpreted as representing progress.

It is one of the most recognisable features of Pakistan's flag.

The five-pointed star

The white five-pointed star is another traditional Islamic symbol and is commonly associated with light and knowledge.

The crescent and star together give the flag its unmistakable Pakistani identity.

A short history of the region

The territory of modern Pakistan has been home to some of the world's earliest urban civilisations. It later came under the influence of Persian, Greek, Mauryan, Kushan, Islamic, Mughal, Sikh and British powers.

The modern state emerged from the Pakistan Movement and the partition of British India in 1947.

History timeline

c. 2500–1900 BCE — Indus Valley Civilisation

Major cities including Mohenjo-daro and Harappa developed sophisticated urban systems, drainage and long-distance trade.

c. 1500–500 BCE — Vedic and later cultures

The wider Indus and northern South Asian region experienced major cultural and linguistic developments.

6th–5th centuries BCE — Persian influence

Parts of present-day Pakistan came under the Achaemenid Persian Empire.

326 BCE — Alexander the Great

Alexander invaded the northwestern Indian subcontinent and fought the famous Battle of the Hydaspes against King Porus.

3rd century BCE — Mauryan Empire

The region became part of the Mauryan Empire under rulers including Ashoka, whose reign helped spread Buddhism.

1st–3rd centuries CE — Kushan Empire

The Kushans controlled much of the region and helped connect South Asia with Central Asia and the Silk Roads.

8th century — Arab conquest of Sindh

Muhammad bin Qasim conquered Sindh in 711–712 CE, establishing an early permanent Muslim political presence in the region.

11th century — Ghaznavid expansion

Mahmud of Ghazni and his successors controlled parts of the region and expanded Muslim political influence.

13th–16th centuries — Delhi Sultanates

Several Muslim dynasties based in Delhi ruled large parts of northern South Asia.

16th–18th centuries — Mughal Empire

The Mughals built a powerful empire across much of South Asia. Lahore became an important imperial centre.

18th century — Regional powers

Mughal authority declined while Afghan, Sikh and other regional powers competed for control.

1799–1849 — Sikh Empire

Ranjit Singh established a powerful Sikh state centred on Lahore that controlled much of present-day Punjab.

1849 — British annexation of Punjab

Following the Anglo-Sikh Wars, the British East India Company annexed Punjab.

1858 — British Crown rule

After the Indian Rebellion of 1857, authority passed from the East India Company to the British Crown.

1906 — All-India Muslim League founded

The Muslim League was established in Dhaka and later became the principal political organisation behind the demand for Pakistan.

1930 — Allahabad Address

Muhammad Iqbal outlined the idea of a consolidated Muslim-majority political unit in northwestern India.

1940 — Lahore Resolution

The Muslim League adopted a resolution calling for independent states in Muslim-majority areas of British India.

1947 — Pakistan becomes independent

British India was partitioned and Pakistan became independent on 14 August, initially consisting of West and East Pakistan.

1947–1948 — First Indo-Pakistani War

India and Pakistan fought over the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir after partition.

1956 — First constitution

Pakistan became an Islamic republic under its first constitution.

1958 — Military rule begins

General Ayub Khan came to power after a military coup and later became president.

1965 — Second Indo-Pakistani War

India and Pakistan fought another major conflict, largely centred on Kashmir.

1971 — Bangladesh independence

Political and military conflict in East Pakistan led to the creation of Bangladesh after intervention by India.

1971 — Zulfikar Ali Bhutto

Bhutto became president and later prime minister, overseeing Pakistan's political reconstruction after the 1971 crisis.

1973 — Constitution

Pakistan adopted its current constitution, establishing a federal parliamentary republic.

1977 — Zia-ul-Haq takes power

A military coup brought General Zia-ul-Haq to power.

1979 — Soviet invasion of Afghanistan

Pakistan became a major base and partner for the Afghan resistance, with millions of refugees crossing the border.

1988 — Return to civilian government

Following Zia-ul-Haq's death, Benazir Bhutto became prime minister.

1998 — Nuclear tests

Pakistan conducted nuclear weapons tests after India's tests earlier that year.

1999 — Military coup

General Pervez Musharraf took power following a military coup.

2001 — War in Afghanistan

Pakistan became a key partner of the United States after the September 11 attacks, while facing significant domestic security pressures.

2008 — Civilian government returns

Pakistan returned to civilian-led government after elections.

2010 — 18th Constitutional Amendment

A major constitutional reform strengthened parliamentary government and provincial autonomy.

2013 — Democratic transfer of power

For the first time in Pakistan's history, one elected civilian government completed a full term and transferred power to another elected government.

2018 — General election

Imran Khan and the Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf party formed the federal government.

2022 — Imran Khan removed

A parliamentary vote of no confidence removed Imran Khan as prime minister and Shehbaz Sharif became prime minister.

2022 — Devastating floods

Exceptional monsoon flooding affected millions of people and caused enormous damage across Pakistan.

2023 — Economic and political pressure

Pakistan faced severe inflation, foreign-exchange pressures and political uncertainty.

2024 — General election

A national election was held amid intense political competition and controversy. Shehbaz Sharif returned as prime minister.

Today

Pakistan remains a large federal republic facing economic, political, environmental and security challenges while retaining major strategic importance in South Asia.

The Indus Valley Civilisation

The Indus Valley Civilisation was one of the world's earliest urban civilisations. Major sites such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa contained planned streets, sophisticated drainage and evidence of long-distance trade.

Mohenjo-daro is now a UNESCO World Heritage site and remains one of Pakistan's most important archaeological landmarks.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah

Muhammad Ali Jinnah was the principal leader of the Pakistan Movement and became Pakistan's first Governor-General in 1947.

Known as Quaid-e-Azam, meaning 'Great Leader', Jinnah played the central political role in negotiating the creation of Pakistan.

The Pakistan Movement

The Pakistan Movement was the political campaign that sought a separate homeland for Muslims in British India.

The All-India Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, became the movement's dominant political organisation. The Lahore Resolution of 1940 was a major turning point.

The Lahore Resolution

The Lahore Resolution of 1940 was adopted by the Muslim League at its annual session in Lahore.

It called for independent states in Muslim-majority areas of northwestern and eastern British India. It later became closely associated with the demand for Pakistan.

Partition and independence

British India was partitioned in August 1947, creating India and Pakistan. Partition triggered one of the largest mass migrations in modern history.

Millions of Muslims, Hindus and Sikhs moved across the new borders, while widespread communal violence caused enormous loss of life.

East and West Pakistan

At independence, Pakistan consisted of two geographically separated wings: West Pakistan and East Pakistan, with India between them.

Political, economic and linguistic tensions between the two wings eventually contributed to the 1971 crisis and the creation of Bangladesh.

The creation of Bangladesh

In 1971, conflict between the Pakistani government and Bengali political forces in East Pakistan escalated into war. India intervened militarily, and East Pakistan became independent Bangladesh.

The loss of East Pakistan was a defining event in Pakistan's history and led to major political change in the remaining country.

Pakistan and Kashmir

The status of Jammu and Kashmir has been disputed between India and Pakistan since partition in 1947.

The two countries have fought several wars and major military confrontations over the region. Kashmir remains one of the most sensitive issues in South Asian politics.

Pakistan's nuclear programme

Pakistan developed nuclear weapons partly in response to regional security concerns and India's nuclear programme. Pakistan conducted nuclear tests in May 1998.

Pakistan is now one of the world's nuclear-armed states and maintains a significant military capability.

Pakistan and Afghanistan

Pakistan shares a long and strategically important border with Afghanistan.

The relationship has been shaped by the Soviet-Afghan War, refugee movements, militant groups, trade, border disputes and the security situation following the Taliban's return to power in 2021.

The Karakoram

The Karakoram mountain range runs through northern Pakistan and contains some of the world's highest peaks.

K2, at 8,611 metres, is the world's second-highest mountain after Mount Everest. Pakistan's northern mountains attract climbers and trekkers from around the world.

The Himalayas and Hindu Kush

Pakistan contains parts of the Himalayas, Hindu Kush and Karakoram mountain systems.

The mountains provide glaciers and rivers that are vital to agriculture and water supplies across the country.

The Indus River

The Indus River is the geographical and economic backbone of Pakistan. It rises in the Tibetan Plateau and flows through Pakistan before reaching the Arabian Sea.

Its irrigation system supports one of the world's largest agricultural areas and is crucial to food production.

Pakistan's climate

Pakistan has a highly varied climate, ranging from high-altitude mountain climates to hot deserts and humid coastal conditions.

Most of the country is affected by the summer monsoon, while northern and western areas can be much drier. Flooding and drought are recurring risks.

The monsoon

The South Asian monsoon provides a large share of Pakistan's annual rainfall.

Good monsoon rains are essential for agriculture, but exceptionally heavy rainfall can produce catastrophic flooding, as seen in 2022.

Pakistan's wildlife

Pakistan's varied landscapes support snow leopards, Himalayan brown bears, markhor, ibex, Indus river dolphins, crocodiles and many bird species.

The markhor, a wild goat with distinctive spiral horns, is Pakistan's national animal.

The markhor

The markhor is Pakistan's national animal and is found in mountainous areas.

Its name comes from Persian and is commonly interpreted as relating to a snake or serpent, reflecting the animal's distinctive spiral horns.

Pakistan's culture

Pakistan has a diverse culture shaped by South Asian, Islamic, Persian, Central Asian and regional traditions.

Music, poetry, literature, cricket, film, architecture, embroidery and regional festivals all contribute to the country's cultural identity.

The languages of Pakistan

Pakistan is linguistically diverse. Urdu is the national language and English is widely used in government, law and higher education.

Major regional languages include Punjabi, Sindhi, Pashto, Balochi and Saraiki, alongside many smaller languages.

Pakistani food

Pakistani cuisine includes biryani, nihari, karahi, kebabs, haleem, chapati, naan and a huge variety of regional dishes.

Food traditions vary considerably between Punjab, Sindh, Balochistan, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and the northern mountain regions.

Cricket in Pakistan

Cricket is Pakistan's most popular sport and a major part of national culture.

Pakistan won the Cricket World Cup in 1992 and the T20 World Cup in 2009. The national team has produced internationally famous players and fast bowlers.

The Pakistani economy

Pakistan has a large and diverse economy with major sectors including agriculture, textiles, manufacturing, services, construction and telecommunications.

Remittances from Pakistanis working overseas are also an important source of foreign exchange.

Agriculture

Agriculture remains important to Pakistan's economy and rural population. Major products include wheat, rice, cotton, sugarcane, maize and fruit.

The Indus irrigation system is essential to farming but also leaves the country vulnerable to water shortages and flood damage.

Textiles

Pakistan has a major textile and clothing industry, with cotton production providing an important raw material.

Textiles are among the country's leading export industries and employ large numbers of workers.

Islamabad

Islamabad became Pakistan's capital in the 1960s, replacing Rawalpindi as the temporary capital.

The city was purpose-built with extensive green spaces and lies beneath the Margalla Hills. The Faisal Mosque is one of its best-known landmarks.

Karachi

Karachi is Pakistan's largest city and its principal commercial and financial centre.

Its port, industrial base and location on the Arabian Sea make it vital to the national economy.

Lahore

Lahore is one of Pakistan's major cultural and historic centres. It was an important Mughal city and contains landmarks including the Lahore Fort and Badshahi Mosque.

The city is also famous for literature, food, gardens, education and the Lahore Resolution of 1940.

Pakistan today

Pakistan is a federal parliamentary republic with a population of more than 240 million. It has a strategically important position between South Asia, Central Asia, China and the Middle East.

The country faces major challenges including economic stability, political polarisation, climate change, water security, energy needs and internal security, while also possessing significant human, agricultural and industrial resources.

Why is Pakistan's flag interesting?

Pakistan's flag is particularly distinctive because the white crescent and star sit on a dark green field beside a separate white stripe.

It tells a compact story about Pakistan's founding: Islam, the Muslim majority, religious minorities and the political movement that led to independence in 1947.
