About Micronesia

The Federated States of Micronesia (FSM) is an island country spread across the western Pacific Ocean. It is made up of four states — Yap, Chuuk, Pohnpei and Kosrae — containing hundreds of islands and atolls scattered across a huge area of ocean.

Palikir, on the island of Pohnpei, is the national capital. The country's geography has shaped its culture, traditional navigation, economy and close relationship with the sea.

The flag of Micronesia

The flag of the Federated States of Micronesia has a light blue field with four white five-pointed stars arranged in a diamond pattern.

The four stars represent the four states of the federation: Chuuk, Kosrae, Pohnpei and Yap.
The light blue background represents the Pacific Ocean.

The design deliberately echoes the blue-and-star imagery of the wider Pacific region while representing the country's federal structure.

The light blue field

The light blue field represents the Pacific Ocean that surrounds and connects the islands of Micronesia.

The colour also gives the flag a strong visual connection to the country's identity as a Pacific island federation.

The four stars

The flag's four white stars represent the four states of the Federated States of Micronesia: Chuuk, Kosrae, Pohnpei and Yap.

The stars are arranged in a diamond, reflecting the geographical and political relationship between the four states.

The four states

The FSM consists of Chuuk, Kosrae, Pohnpei and Yap. Each state has its own islands, communities, traditions and local government.

Pohnpei contains the capital, Palikir, while Chuuk is famous for its lagoon and sunken wartime ships. Yap is renowned for its traditional culture and stone money, and Kosrae is known for its forests and ancient ruins.

A short history of Micronesia

The islands of Micronesia were settled by Pacific peoples thousands of years ago. Communities developed distinctive languages, cultures and systems of navigation long before European contact.

Spain, Germany, Japan and later the United States administered parts of the region. The modern FSM emerged from the former US-administered Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands.

History timeline

Thousands of years ago — Pacific settlement

Ocean-going peoples settled the islands of Micronesia, developing sophisticated maritime cultures adapted to life across widely separated islands.

Ancient era — Island societies develop

Distinct communities and political systems developed across Yap, Chuuk, Pohnpei and Kosrae.

c. 1200–1600 — Nan Madol flourishes

On Pohnpei, the remarkable stone complex of Nan Madol developed as a ceremonial and political centre.

16th century — Spanish contact

Spanish navigators reached and mapped parts of the Caroline Islands, beginning a period of increasing European contact.

19th century — Missionaries and traders

Christian missionaries and foreign traders became increasingly active across the islands.

1899 — Germany acquires the Caroline Islands

Germany purchased Spain's remaining Pacific possessions and administered the Caroline Islands.

1914 — Japanese occupation

Japan took control of the islands during the First World War and later administered them under a League of Nations mandate.

1940s — Second World War

The islands became strategically important during the Pacific War. Chuuk Lagoon in particular became a major Japanese naval base.

1947 — US-administered trust territory

The islands became part of the United Nations Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands, administered by the United States.

1979 — FSM Constitution

The Federated States of Micronesia was established as a constitutional federation.

3 November 1986 — Independence

The Compact of Free Association came into force and the FSM became a sovereign state in free association with the United States.

1990 — UN trust territory ends

The United Nations formally ended the Trust Territory status for the FSM.

1991 — UN membership

The Federated States of Micronesia joined the United Nations.

2004 — Typhoon Sudal

Major storms and natural disasters highlighted the vulnerability of the islands and their infrastructure.

21st century — Climate pressures

Sea-level rise, coastal erosion, extreme weather and changing ocean conditions have become major concerns for the island federation.

Today

The FSM remains a sovereign Pacific island federation with strong constitutional, economic and security links to the United States.

Traditional navigation

Micronesian societies developed some of the world's most sophisticated systems of traditional ocean navigation.

Skilled navigators used stars, ocean swells, winds, clouds, birds and other environmental clues to travel between islands without modern instruments.

Yap

Yap is famous for its traditional culture, including stone money known as rai. These enormous stone discs were quarried on Palau and transported across the sea.

Yap also has distinctive traditional dances, houses and social customs that remain important today.

Chuuk

Chuuk is best known for its huge lagoon and its World War II underwater wrecks. The lagoon contains numerous Japanese ships and aircraft sunk during Operation Hailstone in 1944.

It is now one of the world's best-known destinations for wreck diving.

Pohnpei

Pohnpei is one of the largest and wettest islands in the FSM. It contains the national capital, Palikir, and the ancient stone city of Nan Madol.

The island's mountainous interior is covered by lush tropical vegetation.

Kosrae

Kosrae is the easternmost of the four FSM states. It is heavily forested and known for coral reefs, waterfalls and archaeological sites.

The island has a relatively small population and a strong connection to traditional culture and the natural environment.

Nan Madol

Nan Madol is a remarkable ancient city built on a series of artificial islets off Pohnpei. Its structures were constructed from huge basalt stones and coral.

It served as a ceremonial and political centre associated with the Saudeleur dynasty and is now a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Spanish rule and contact

Spain claimed the Caroline Islands during the age of European expansion. Spanish influence increased in the nineteenth century, particularly through missionaries and administration.

Spain eventually sold its remaining Pacific possessions to Germany in 1899.

German administration

Germany administered the Caroline Islands from 1899 until the First World War. German traders and officials became involved in the islands' copra and commercial economy.

The period ended when Japanese forces took control in 1914.

Japanese administration

Japan administered the Caroline Islands after the First World War under a League of Nations mandate.

Japanese settlement and commercial development increased, and the islands became strategically important during the Second World War.

Chuuk Lagoon in World War II

Chuuk Lagoon was a major Japanese naval base during the Pacific War. In February 1944, US forces launched Operation Hailstone, sinking many ships and destroying aircraft.

The wrecks now form a major underwater archaeological and diving site.

The Trust Territory

After the Second World War, the islands became part of the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands, administered by the United States on behalf of the United Nations.

The territory eventually separated into several independent states, including the Federated States of Micronesia, the Marshall Islands and Palau.

Independence and the Compact of Free Association

The FSM became independent on 3 November 1986 in free association with the United States under the Compact of Free Association.

The arrangement provides close economic and security links while recognising the FSM as a sovereign state.

Palikir

Palikir, on Pohnpei, is the capital of the Federated States of Micronesia. It became the national capital when the federation was established.

The city is relatively small compared with capitals in larger countries, reflecting the FSM's population and dispersed island geography.

Micronesia's geography

The FSM consists of hundreds of islands spread across a huge area of the western Pacific. The islands range from high volcanic islands to low coral atolls.

The distance between islands makes transport, communications and public services particularly challenging.

The Pacific Ocean

The Pacific is central to Micronesian life. The sea provides food, transport, cultural connections and economic opportunities.

At the same time, rising sea levels and stronger coastal impacts from storms create serious risks for low-lying communities.

Micronesia's climate

The FSM has a tropical climate with warm temperatures throughout the year. Rainfall varies between islands and locations.

Tropical storms and typhoons can cause severe damage, while climate change is increasing concerns about coastal flooding and erosion.

Marine life

The waters around the FSM contain coral reefs, reef fish, sharks, turtles, dolphins and other marine life.

Healthy reefs are important for both biodiversity and local communities that depend on fishing and tourism.

Micronesian culture

Micronesian culture is highly diverse. Each island group has its own languages, traditions, dances, crafts, food and social customs.

Community relationships, family networks and connections to land and sea remain central to everyday life.

Languages

The FSM is linguistically diverse, with many Micronesian and other Austronesian languages spoken across the islands. English is the official language of the federal government.

Local languages remain essential to cultural identity and are widely used within communities.

Food

Traditional Micronesian food relies heavily on fish, taro, breadfruit, coconut, bananas and other tropical crops.

Food traditions vary by island and reflect the availability of local marine and agricultural resources.

Stone money of Yap

Yap's rai stones are huge circular limestone discs with a hole in the centre. Some are several metres across and can weigh several tonnes.

The stones traditionally represented wealth and social status. Ownership could change even when the stone itself remained in place.

Micronesia's economy

The FSM economy relies on government services, fisheries, agriculture, tourism, remittances and financial assistance connected to the Compact of Free Association.

Its dispersed geography makes large-scale manufacturing difficult, while sustainable management of marine resources is economically important.

Fishing

Fishing is important for both subsistence and commercial activity. Tuna and other pelagic fish are valuable marine resources in the wider Pacific.

Managing fisheries sustainably is important for food security and long-term economic development.

Tourism

Tourism is based around diving, reefs, wrecks, fishing, nature and cultural experiences.

Chuuk's wartime wrecks and the natural landscapes of Pohnpei, Kosrae and Yap attract visitors looking for less-developed Pacific destinations.

Climate change

Climate change is a major concern for the FSM. Sea-level rise, coastal erosion, coral bleaching and extreme weather can threaten homes, infrastructure and food systems.

Because communities are spread across small islands, adaptation and resilience are particularly important.

Micronesia today

The Federated States of Micronesia remains a sovereign four-state federation with a close relationship with the United States.

Its greatest strengths include rich marine ecosystems, distinctive island cultures and strong community traditions. Its major challenges include geographic isolation, climate change, transport costs and limited economic opportunities.

Why is Micronesia's flag interesting?

The FSM flag is simple but highly meaningful: one blue field represents the ocean and four stars represent the four states.

It is a good example of how a national flag can express both geography and political structure without using a complicated coat of arms.
