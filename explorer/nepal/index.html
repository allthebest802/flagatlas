About Nepal

Nepal is a landlocked country in South Asia between India and China. Much of the country is mountainous, and the Himalayas contain some of the world's highest peaks.

Kathmandu is the capital and the centre of Nepal's political and cultural life. Nepal is famous for Mount Everest, the Kathmandu Valley, Hindu and Buddhist heritage, trekking and extraordinary geographical diversity.

The flag of Nepal

Nepal has the only national flag in the world that is not rectangular. It consists of two stacked triangular pennons with a deep crimson field, blue borders and white symbols of the moon and sun.

The flag's shape is closely associated with the Himalayan and South Asian flag traditions of Nepal.
The crimson red is Nepal's national colour, the blue border is associated with peace and harmony, and the sun and moon represent permanence and endurance.

The current constitutional form of the flag was established in 1962, following the adoption of Nepal's new constitution.

The crimson red field

The crimson red is Nepal's national colour and is also associated with the country's national flower, the rhododendron.

The red field gives the flag its strong visual identity and contrasts with the blue border and white celestial symbols.

The blue border

The blue border represents peace and harmony.

It frames the two red pennons and provides a distinctive contrast with the white moon and sun.

The moon

The upper pennon contains a white crescent moon. It is traditionally associated with the cool Himalayan climate and the aspiration that Nepal will remain peaceful and enduring.

The moon also contributes to the flag's distinctive visual connection with celestial symbols.

The sun

The lower pennon contains a white stylised sun. The sun is associated with heat, energy and endurance.

Together, the moon and sun express the traditional idea that Nepal should endure for as long as these celestial bodies exist.

Why is Nepal's flag triangular?

Nepal's unusual shape is linked to traditional pennant forms used in South Asia and the Himalayan region. The two triangles are sometimes interpreted as representing the Himalayan mountains.

The design was preserved when the modern national flag was standardised, making Nepal instantly recognisable around the world.

A short history of Nepal

Nepal's history developed around the Kathmandu Valley and the Himalayan trade routes between the Indian subcontinent and Tibet. The region was home to powerful city-states and dynasties before Prithvi Narayan Shah unified much of the country in the eighteenth century.

Nepal later fought the British, established a hereditary Rana regime and eventually became a constitutional monarchy before transforming into a federal democratic republic in 2008.

History timeline

Ancient era — Himalayan and South Asian cultures

The region of modern Nepal was inhabited by diverse peoples and influenced by both the Indian subcontinent and Himalayan trade routes.

c. 6th century BC — Birth of Siddhartha Gautama

Siddhartha Gautama, who became the Buddha, was born at Lumbini in the region of modern Nepal.

3rd century BC — Mauryan influence

Emperor Ashoka of the Mauryan Empire is traditionally associated with pilgrimage to Lumbini and the spread of Buddhism.

Licchavi period

The Licchavi era saw the development of important political, artistic and religious traditions in the Kathmandu Valley.

12th–18th centuries — Malla period

Malla rulers developed the Kathmandu Valley's three great urban centres: Kathmandu, Patan and Bhaktapur.

15th century — Kathmandu Valley city culture

Trade, architecture, metalwork and religious art flourished in the valley's city-states.

1768–1769 — Unification of Nepal

Prithvi Narayan Shah conquered Kathmandu Valley and laid the foundations of the unified Kingdom of Nepal.

1814–1816 — Anglo-Nepalese War

Nepal fought the British East India Company. The Treaty of Sugauli established the post-war frontier and reduced Nepal's territorial ambitions.

1846 — Rana regime begins

Jung Bahadur Rana seized power and established a hereditary Rana prime-ministerial regime that dominated Nepal for more than a century.

1923 — Britain recognises Nepal's independence

The United Kingdom formally recognised Nepal as an independent and sovereign state.

1950–1951 — End of Rana rule

Political change restored the monarchy's active role and began Nepal's transition towards parliamentary government.

1955 — Nepal joins the United Nations

Nepal became a member of the United Nations and expanded its international diplomatic relationships.

1960 — Royal takeover

King Mahendra dismissed the elected government and established the partyless Panchayat system.

1990 — Democratic movement

Mass protests led to the restoration of multiparty democracy and a constitutional monarchy.

1996 — Maoist insurgency

The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) launched an armed rebellion that became a major national conflict.

2001 — Royal massacre

King Birendra and several members of the royal family were killed in a massacre at the royal palace. Gyanendra subsequently became king.

2005 — Direct royal rule

King Gyanendra assumed direct political power, intensifying opposition to the monarchy.

2006 — Peace agreement

The Comprehensive Peace Accord ended the armed conflict and opened the way for a new political settlement.

2008 — Republic declared

Nepal abolished its monarchy and became a federal democratic republic.

2015 — New constitution

Nepal adopted a new constitution establishing a federal democratic republican system.

2015 — Earthquake

A devastating earthquake struck central Nepal, causing major loss of life and extensive damage, including to historic heritage sites.

Today

Nepal is a federal democratic republic whose economy relies heavily on services, tourism, agriculture and remittances.

Lumbini and the Buddha

Lumbini in southern Nepal is traditionally recognised as the birthplace of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha.

The sacred site attracts Buddhist pilgrims from around the world and is one of Nepal's most important religious and cultural landmarks.

The Kathmandu Valley

The Kathmandu Valley has been a major centre of Himalayan civilisation for centuries. Its historic cities include Kathmandu, Patan and Bhaktapur.

The valley is famous for temples, stupas, palaces, courtyards, wood carving and metalwork shaped by Hindu and Buddhist traditions.

The Malla kings

The Malla dynasty played a major role in developing the Kathmandu Valley's urban and artistic culture.

Although the valley was politically divided between rival city-states, competition between the rulers encouraged extraordinary temple and palace construction.

Prithvi Narayan Shah

Prithvi Narayan Shah, king of Gorkha, conquered the Kathmandu Valley in the eighteenth century and created the foundations of modern Nepal.

His campaigns brought many Himalayan territories together under one expanding kingdom.

The Anglo-Nepalese War

The Anglo-Nepalese War of 1814–1816 was fought between Nepal and the British East India Company.

Nepal retained its independence, but the Treaty of Sugauli reduced its territory and established much of the basis of its later borders.

The Gurkhas

Nepal's Gurkha soldiers became internationally famous for their service in the British and Indian armies and other military forces.

Their reputation for courage and discipline developed through a long history of military service and remains an important part of Nepal's international identity.

The Rana regime

The Rana regime began in 1846 when Jung Bahadur Rana established hereditary control of the prime ministership.

The Ranas dominated Nepal's political system while the monarchy's practical authority was greatly reduced. The regime ended in the early 1950s.

The 1990 democratic movement

The 1990 People's Movement forced King Birendra to restore multiparty democracy.

A new constitutional monarchy was established, beginning a period of parliamentary politics that later gave way to the Maoist conflict and another major political transformation.

The Maoist conflict

Nepal's Maoist insurgency lasted from 1996 to 2006 and caused thousands of deaths and widespread disruption.

The conflict ended with a peace agreement that opened the way for elections, a new constitution and the eventual abolition of the monarchy.

The 2001 royal massacre

In June 2001, King Birendra and several members of the royal family were killed at Narayanhiti Palace.

The tragedy profoundly affected Nepal's political future and was followed by the accession of King Gyanendra.

The 2008 republic

Nepal's monarchy was formally abolished in 2008 after elections to a Constituent Assembly.

The country became a federal democratic republic, marking the end of a monarchy with centuries of history.

Kathmandu

Kathmandu is Nepal's capital and largest urban centre. It sits in the Kathmandu Valley and is the country's main political, economic and cultural hub.

The city combines ancient temples and courtyards with modern offices, businesses and dense urban development.

Mount Everest

Mount Everest, known in Nepal as Sagarmatha, is the world's highest mountain above sea level.

The mountain stands on the Nepal–China border and is one of the world's most famous symbols of the Himalayas and Nepal.

The Himalayas

The Himalayas dominate northern Nepal and contain many of the world's highest peaks.

Mountain environments range from subtropical foothills to permanent snow and ice, creating enormous biodiversity and challenging conditions for transport and settlement.

Pokhara

Pokhara is a major tourist city beside Phewa Lake and is known for spectacular views of the Annapurna range.

It is one of Nepal's main bases for trekking, mountaineering, paragliding and other outdoor activities.

Chitwan National Park

Chitwan National Park protects forests, grasslands and wetlands in southern Nepal.

It is famous for one-horned rhinoceroses, Bengal tigers, elephants, crocodiles and many bird species.

Nepal's climate

Nepal has an extremely varied climate because of its huge differences in altitude. The country ranges from subtropical lowlands to alpine and Himalayan conditions.

The summer monsoon brings most of the country's rainfall, while high mountain areas remain cold and snow-covered for much of the year.

The 2015 earthquake

A major earthquake struck Nepal on 25 April 2015, followed by further damaging tremors.

Thousands of people died and historic buildings, villages and infrastructure were badly damaged, including important heritage sites in the Kathmandu Valley.

Nepal's wildlife

Nepal supports remarkable wildlife including Bengal tigers, one-horned rhinoceroses, red pandas, snow leopards, Himalayan black bears and wild elephants.

The country's national parks and conservation areas protect habitats ranging from tropical forests to high Himalayan landscapes.

Hinduism and Buddhism

Nepal has a strong religious heritage shaped especially by Hinduism and Buddhism.

Temples, monasteries, shrines and festivals often reflect interaction between the two traditions, particularly in the Kathmandu Valley.

Nepali culture

Nepali culture is highly diverse, reflecting Himalayan, South Asian, Tibetan and indigenous traditions.

Music, dance, festivals, architecture, crafts and food vary between ethnic communities and geographical regions.

Nepali language

Nepali is the official language and is written mainly in the Devanagari script.

Nepal is multilingual, with many other languages spoken by communities across the Terai, hills and mountains.

Nepali food

Nepali cuisine includes dal bhat, momo dumplings, thukpa, sel roti and a wide range of regional dishes.

Rice, lentils, vegetables, spices and pickles are common ingredients, while Tibetan and Himalayan influences are especially visible in mountain cuisine.

Tourism

Tourism is one of Nepal's most important industries. Visitors come for trekking, mountaineering, pilgrimage, wildlife and cultural tourism.

The Everest region, Annapurna, Pokhara, Chitwan and the Kathmandu Valley are among the country's best-known destinations.

Nepal's economy

Nepal's economy relies on services, agriculture, tourism, manufacturing and remittances.

Millions of Nepalese work abroad, making remittances a major source of household income and foreign exchange.

Hydropower

Nepal has enormous potential for hydroelectric power because of its steep mountain rivers.

Hydropower investment has expanded, although geography, infrastructure and financing make large projects challenging.

Nepal today

Modern Nepal is a young federal republic balancing rapid urbanisation with a predominantly rural population and challenging mountain geography.

Major issues include infrastructure, employment, migration, disaster resilience, climate change and protecting the country's exceptional natural and cultural heritage.

Why is Nepal's flag interesting?

Nepal's flag is the only current national flag that is not rectangular. Its two triangular pennons, blue border and white moon and sun make it instantly recognisable.

It is also unusual because its design combines national colour, celestial symbolism and a shape rooted in Nepal's historic flag traditions.
