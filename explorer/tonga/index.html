About Tonga

Tonga is a Polynesian kingdom in the South Pacific Ocean, made up of more than 170 islands, although only a small number are inhabited. It is famous for its coral islands, traditional Polynesian culture, humpback whales and the fact that it is one of the world's few remaining monarchies.

The Tonga flag

The Tongan flag has a red field with a white canton in the upper hoist. Inside the canton is a prominent red cross, making the design strongly associated with Christianity and the country's history.

Flag colours and symbolism

Red represents the blood of Jesus Christ, while white represents purity. The red cross reflects the importance of Christianity in Tongan society.

Flag history

Tonga's current flag dates from the 19th century and is associated with King George Tupou I and the country's adoption of Christianity. A similar design was used before the modern constitutional period, although its precise legal history has changed over time.

A short history

Tonga has a long Polynesian history and developed powerful chiefly traditions and maritime connections across the Pacific. Christian missionaries arrived in the 19th century, followed by the unification of Tonga under George Tupou I. Tonga became a British-protected state but retained its monarchy and internal self-government, becoming fully independent in 1970.

History timeline

Ancient Polynesia — SettlementTongans developed communities across the islands and built strong maritime links with other parts of Polynesia.

17th–18th centuries — European contactEuropean explorers visited the islands, although Tonga retained its own political structures.
1820s–1840s — Christian conversionChristianity spread rapidly and became deeply integrated into Tongan society.
1845 — George Tupou ITaufaʻahau united Tonga and became King George Tupou I, laying the foundations of the modern Tongan state.
1875 — ConstitutionTonga adopted a constitution and established a modern constitutional monarchy.
1900 — British protected stateTonga entered into a treaty relationship with Britain while retaining its monarchy and internal administration.
1970 — Full independenceTonga ended its protected-state relationship with Britain and became fully independent.

Nuku'alofa

Nuku'alofa is Tonga's capital and largest urban centre. It lies on the north coast of Tongatapu and is the country's political, commercial and transport hub.

Tongatapu

Tongatapu is Tonga's largest and most populous island and the centre of national government. Much of the island is low-lying and made up of uplifted coral limestone.

The Haʻamonga ʻa Maui

The Haʻamonga ʻa Maui is a huge trilithon of coral limestone on Tongatapu. It is one of Tonga's best-known archaeological monuments and is traditionally linked with the ancient Tuʻi Tonga dynasty.

Geography

Tonga consists of a long chain of islands and islets spread across the South Pacific. The islands include volcanic islands and low coral atolls, and the country lies east of Fiji and west of Samoa.

Climate

Tonga has a tropical maritime climate. Temperatures remain warm throughout the year, with a wetter season and a drier season. Tropical cyclones can affect the islands, particularly during the Southern Hemisphere summer.

Volcanoes and coral islands

Tonga sits near the Tonga-Kermadec subduction zone, one of the most active tectonic regions on Earth. The kingdom contains both volcanic islands and low-lying coral islands.

Humpback whales

Tonga is famous for its seasonal population of humpback whales. Whales migrate to the warm waters around Tonga to breed and give birth, making whale watching an important part of tourism.

Marine life

Tonga's waters support coral reefs, sea turtles, dolphins, tropical fish and other marine species. The health of these ecosystems is important to local communities and tourism.

Tongan culture

Tongan society places strong importance on family, community, respect and traditional hierarchy. Ceremonies, dance, music, weaving and elaborate feasts remain important parts of cultural life.

Language

Tongan is the main national language and is closely related to other Polynesian languages. English is also widely used, particularly in education, government and business.

Religion

Christianity is central to Tongan society, with several Christian denominations represented. Sunday is traditionally treated as a particularly important day of worship and rest.

Traditional dance and music

Traditional Tongan performance includes lakalaka, a formal group dance involving singing, movement and carefully coordinated gestures. It is an important part of national cultural heritage.

Food

Tongan food commonly includes root crops, coconut, fish, pork and taro. Traditional feasts may feature underground-oven cooking known as an umu.

Economy

Tonga's economy depends on agriculture, fishing, tourism, remittances and services. Because of its small population and remote location, overseas remittances are particularly important.

Agriculture

Important crops include coconuts, squash, taro, yams and bananas. Tonga also exports agricultural produce, although farming is vulnerable to cyclones and other extreme weather.

The monarchy

Tonga is a constitutional monarchy and one of the few remaining monarchies in the Pacific. The modern Tongan monarchy traces its roots to the unification of the islands under George Tupou I.

Why is Tonga's flag interesting?

Tonga's flag is one of the world's oldest continuously used national flags. Its simple red-and-white design and cross reflect the country's strong Christian heritage and its long history as a Polynesian kingdom.
