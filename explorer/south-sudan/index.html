About South Sudan

South Sudan is a landlocked country in East-Central Africa and the world's youngest internationally recognised sovereign state. It became independent from Sudan on 9 July 2011.

The country is dominated by vast grasslands, wetlands and the Nile basin. Its capital and largest city is Juba.

The South Sudan flag

The South Sudan flag has horizontal black, red and green bands, separated by narrow white stripes. A blue triangle at the hoist contains a gold five-pointed star.

The flag was adopted in 2011 when South Sudan became independent. It was based on the flag used by the Sudan People's Liberation Movement during the struggle for self-determination.

Black

The black band represents the people of South Sudan. It forms the upper horizontal stripe of the flag and is separated from the red band by a narrow white line.

Red

The red band represents the blood shed during the long struggle for freedom and independence. The colour is a reminder of the enormous human cost of South Sudan's wars.

Green

The green band represents the land and the country's fertile agricultural areas. South Sudan contains extensive grasslands, wetlands and river systems.

White

The narrow white stripes represent peace. Their position between the black, red and green bands also gives the flag its distinctive layered appearance.

Blue

The blue triangle represents the waters of the Nile. The White Nile and its tributaries are fundamental to South Sudan's geography, wildlife, agriculture and communities.

The gold star

The gold five-pointed star represents the unity of the states of South Sudan. It is sometimes described as the Star of Unity and stands prominently against the blue triangle.

Why the flag is distinctive

South Sudan's flag combines the familiar Pan-African colours of black, red and green with white, a blue triangle and a gold star. Its symbolism is closely connected with the country's independence struggle and geography.

A short history

The region that is now South Sudan has been home to diverse peoples and kingdoms for centuries. British and Egyptian rule later brought the region into the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, while tensions between northern and southern Sudanese communities contributed to long periods of conflict.

After decades of civil war, a peace agreement led to a referendum in 2011. An overwhelming majority voted for independence, and South Sudan became independent on 9 July 2011.

History timeline

Ancient and medieval periods — Diverse societies

The region developed numerous Nilotic, Nilo-Saharan and other communities, with distinct languages, traditions and political systems.

19th century — Egyptian and British expansion

The territory became increasingly incorporated into Egyptian and later Anglo-Egyptian administration.

1899–1956 — Anglo-Egyptian Sudan

Sudan was governed jointly by Britain and Egypt, with significant differences developing between northern and southern regions.

1955 — First Sudanese civil war begins

Mutiny by southern troops contributed to the outbreak of the first major civil war between northern and southern Sudanese forces.

1956 — Sudanese independence

Sudan became independent from Anglo-Egyptian rule, but tensions between the north and south continued.

1972 — Addis Ababa Agreement

An agreement granted southern Sudan a degree of regional autonomy and brought the first civil war to an end.

1983 — Second Sudanese civil war

War resumed after the Sudanese government abolished southern autonomy and introduced policies that intensified opposition in the south.

1983 — SPLM/SPLA formed

The Sudan People's Liberation Movement and its armed wing became the principal force fighting for southern autonomy and later independence.

2005 — Comprehensive Peace Agreement

The north and south signed a major peace deal that ended the second civil war and provided for a future referendum on independence.

2011 — Independence

After a referendum in which an overwhelming majority voted for independence, South Sudan became independent on 9 July 2011.

2013 — Civil war begins

Political rivalry within the new government developed into a major armed conflict, causing displacement and a severe humanitarian crisis.

2018 — Revitalised peace agreement

A new peace agreement was signed to end the civil war and create a framework for a unity government.

2020 — Transitional unity government

A transitional government was formed, bringing rival political groups into a shared administration.

2024 — Flooding and humanitarian pressures

Severe flooding, displacement, food insecurity and regional instability continued to challenge communities.

Today

South Sudan continues state-building and peace efforts while relying heavily on oil revenues and facing major humanitarian, infrastructure and climate challenges.

Juba

Juba is the capital and largest city of South Sudan. It lies on the White Nile and has developed rapidly since independence, becoming the country's main political, commercial and transport centre.

The White Nile

The White Nile runs through South Sudan and is one of the country's defining geographical features. It provides water, transport routes, fisheries and habitats for wildlife.

The Sudd

The Sudd is one of the world's largest tropical wetlands. Formed largely by the White Nile, it contains vast areas of papyrus, grasses and shallow water and supports extraordinary wildlife diversity.

Climate

South Sudan has a predominantly tropical climate, with a wet season and a dry season. Rainfall generally increases towards the south and decreases towards the north.

Wildlife

South Sudan supports large populations of elephants, giraffes, antelope, buffalo, hippos, crocodiles and many bird species. Its great migrations of antelope are among Africa's most spectacular wildlife events.

The great wildlife migration

The White-eared kob migration and movements of tiang and Mongalla gazelle can involve huge numbers of animals travelling through the country's grasslands. These migrations are comparable in scale to better-known wildlife movements elsewhere in East Africa.

Birdlife

South Sudan's wetlands and grasslands support enormous numbers of birds, including pelicans, storks, herons, egrets, shoebills and many migratory species.

The shoebill

The strange-looking shoebill is one of South Sudan's most famous birds. It lives in wetlands and is known for its huge shoe-shaped bill and prehistoric appearance.

Economy

South Sudan's economy is heavily dependent on oil. Agriculture, livestock and small-scale trade are also important, but infrastructure and decades of conflict have limited broader economic development.

Oil

Oil provides the majority of South Sudan's government revenue and export earnings. Production is concentrated mainly in the northern and eastern parts of the country, with exports transported through pipelines via Sudan.

Agriculture

Most rural households depend to some degree on farming, fishing and livestock. Crops include sorghum, maize, millet, cassava, groundnuts and vegetables.

Livestock

Cattle have major economic and cultural importance in many South Sudanese communities. Livestock ownership can influence wealth, social status, marriage traditions and community relationships.

Culture

South Sudan is extremely culturally diverse, with many ethnic communities and languages. Important traditions include cattle keeping, dance, music, oral storytelling, beadwork and community ceremonies.

Languages

English is the official language, while Juba Arabic is widely used as a lingua franca. South Sudan is home to many indigenous languages, reflecting its extraordinary cultural diversity.

Music and dance

Traditional music and dance vary widely between communities. Drums, singing and group dances are commonly used at ceremonies, celebrations and community events.

Food

South Sudanese food commonly includes sorghum, maize, cassava, beans, okra, greens, fish and meat. Cooking traditions vary between regions and communities.

Religion

South Sudan has a diverse religious landscape including Christianity, traditional African religions and Islam. Christian denominations are particularly widespread.

Education

Education has been severely affected by conflict and displacement. Expanding schools, teacher training and access to education remains one of the country's major development priorities.

Transport

Transport infrastructure is limited, with many roads becoming difficult or impassable during the rainy season. Rivers, air transport and major roads around Juba are therefore particularly important.

Climate change

South Sudan is highly vulnerable to flooding, drought, changing rainfall and extreme heat. Climate shocks can damage crops, displace communities and increase pressure on food and water supplies.

Humanitarian challenges

Conflict, displacement, flooding, food insecurity and limited infrastructure have created major humanitarian needs. Large numbers of South Sudanese people have also sought refuge in neighbouring countries.

National identity

South Sudan's flag is closely tied to the country's independence movement. Its colours tell a story of people, sacrifice, land, peace, water and unity, making it one of the country's most important national symbols.

Why is South Sudan's flag interesting?

South Sudan's flag is relatively new, but its symbolism is deeply rooted in the country's history. The gold star on the blue Nile triangle is particularly distinctive, while the black, red and green bands connect the design to the wider Pan-African tradition.
