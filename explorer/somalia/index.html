About Somalia

Somalia is a country on the Horn of Africa, with a long coastline along the Gulf of Aden and Indian Ocean. It occupies a strategically important position between Africa and the Arabian Peninsula.

Somalia has a rich history of ancient trading centres, Islamic scholarship, pastoral traditions and maritime commerce. Its capital and largest city is Mogadishu.

The Somalia flag

The Somali flag is a simple light blue field with a white five-pointed star at its centre. It was adopted on 12 October 1954 and became the flag of the independent Somali Republic in 1960.

The light blue was historically associated with the United Nations, while the star became a powerful symbol of Somali national identity.

Light blue

The light blue field is traditionally associated with the sky and the surrounding waters. The colour was also chosen to honour the United Nations, which administered the former Italian Somaliland territory during the transition towards independence.

The five-pointed star

The white five-pointed star, known as the Star of Unity, represents the idea of Somali unity. Its five points have traditionally been associated with the major Somali-inhabited territories: former British Somaliland, former Italian Somaliland, the Ogaden, the Northern Frontier District and Djibouti.

The Star of Unity

The central star is more than decoration: it represents the aspiration for Somali unity across territories where Somali people live. This is one reason the star has such strong political and cultural significance.

Why the flag is distinctive

Unlike many national flags, Somalia's flag uses just two main colours and a single central symbol. The uncluttered design makes the white star on blue immediately recognisable.

A short history

Somalia's history includes ancient coastal trading networks, Islamic sultanates and strong links with Arabia, Persia and the wider Indian Ocean. European powers later divided Somali-inhabited territories between different colonial administrations.

Italian Somaliland and British Somaliland followed different colonial paths. In 1960, the two territories united to form the independent Somali Republic. Political instability and civil conflict later reshaped the country, while Somaliland declared itself independent in 1991 but remains internationally unrecognised as a separate state.

History timeline

Ancient era — Indian Ocean trade

Somali coastal settlements participated in long-distance trade involving frankincense, livestock, spices and other goods.

7th–10th centuries — Growth of Islam

Islam spread through the Somali coast and hinterland, strengthening links with Arabia and the wider Muslim world.

Medieval period — Coastal sultanates

Powerful Islamic states and trading centres developed along the Somali coast and inland regions.

16th century — Adal Sultanate

The Adal Sultanate became a major regional power in the Horn of Africa and fought against the Ethiopian Empire.

19th century — European expansion

Britain and Italy established colonial territories in Somali-inhabited lands, while Ethiopia expanded into the Ogaden.

1920s — Consolidation of colonial rule

Italian and British administrations strengthened their control over their respective Somali territories.

1941 — British military administration

During the Second World War British forces took control of Italian Somaliland.

1950 — UN trusteeship

Former Italian Somaliland became a United Nations Trust Territory under Italian administration, with independence planned for 1960.

1954 — Flag adopted

The light blue flag with the white Star of Unity was adopted for the Somali National League and subsequently became the national flag.

1960 — Independence and union

British Somaliland became independent on 26 June, followed by the independence of Italian Somaliland on 1 July. The two territories united to form the Somali Republic.

1969 — Military coup

General Mohamed Siad Barre seized power following the assassination of President Abdirashid Ali Shermarke.

1977–1978 — Ogaden War

Somalia fought Ethiopia over the Ogaden region. Ethiopia, supported by the Soviet Union and Cuba, eventually repelled Somali forces.

1991 — Government collapses

Siad Barre was overthrown. Civil war and political fragmentation followed, while Somaliland declared independence.

2000 — Transitional government

A Transitional National Government was established as part of efforts to rebuild national institutions.

2012 — Federal government

A new federal government was established, marking a major stage in the rebuilding of Somalia's state institutions.

Today

Somalia continues to rebuild its institutions and economy while facing security, humanitarian and climate-related challenges.

Mogadishu

Mogadishu is Somalia's capital and largest city. Located on the Indian Ocean coast, it has been an important trading port for centuries and contains historic mosques, markets and coastal districts.

The Horn of Africa

Somalia occupies much of the eastern tip of Africa known as the Horn of Africa. Its location gives it access to major maritime routes linking the Indian Ocean, Red Sea and Gulf of Aden.

Somali coastline

Somalia has the longest coastline of any country on mainland Africa. The coast includes sandy beaches, coral habitats, mangroves and productive fishing waters.

Climate

Much of Somalia has a hot, dry climate. Rainfall is highly variable and many areas experience recurring droughts, while the southern regions generally receive more rainfall than the far north.

Deserts and semi-deserts

Large areas consist of arid and semi-arid landscapes. Pastoral communities have traditionally adapted to these conditions by moving livestock between grazing and water sources.

Wildlife

Somalia's varied habitats support camels, antelope, gazelles, hyenas, baboons, leopards and many bird species. Marine habitats add turtles, dolphins and a wide variety of fish.

Marine life

Somalia's extensive Indian Ocean coastline supports important fisheries and marine ecosystems. Coastal communities have long depended on fishing alongside pastoral and agricultural livelihoods.

Economy

Somalia's economy includes livestock, agriculture, fisheries, telecommunications, trade and remittances. Livestock exports are particularly important and the country's diaspora contributes significantly through remittances.

Livestock

Livestock production is central to Somali life and the economy. Camels, goats, sheep and cattle are widely kept, with livestock and livestock products important for both domestic livelihoods and exports.

Agriculture

Agriculture is concentrated mainly in the south and along river valleys, where crops such as bananas, maize, sorghum, sesame and vegetables can be grown.

Telecommunications

Somalia has developed a surprisingly dynamic mobile telecommunications sector. Mobile phones and digital payments are widely used, providing important services in a country where conventional infrastructure has been disrupted.

Trade

Somalia's strategic coastline supports trade with neighbouring countries and wider markets across the Red Sea and Indian Ocean. Ports including Mogadishu, Berbera and Kismayo are important to regional commerce.

Culture

Somali culture has strong traditions of poetry, oral storytelling, music, pastoralism, hospitality and clan-based social organisation. Poetry in particular has historically played an unusually important role in public life.

Somali language

Somali is a Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family and is spoken across Somalia and neighbouring parts of the Horn of Africa. Arabic is also an official language and has deep religious and cultural importance.

Poetry

Somali society has a famous oral poetry tradition. Poetry can preserve history, express political ideas, settle disputes and communicate social values, making poets important cultural figures.

Food

Somali cuisine reflects African, Arabian and Indian Ocean influences. Common foods include canjeero, rice, sambusas, meat dishes, camel milk and spiced stews.

Religion

Somalia is overwhelmingly Muslim, with Sunni Islam forming the dominant religious tradition. Islam has influenced Somali law, education, architecture, customs and everyday life for many centuries.

Architecture

Historic Somali architecture includes mosques, coastal trading buildings and settlements influenced by centuries of interaction with Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean. Mogadishu contains some of the country's best-known historic structures.

Education

Somalia's education system has faced major disruption but has expanded in many areas since the 2000s. Universities and private educational institutions operate in several cities, including Mogadishu.

Transport

Roads connect the major population centres, while air transport is particularly important because of Somalia's size and infrastructure challenges. Ports are also essential for trade and coastal communities.

Climate change

Somalia is highly vulnerable to droughts, floods, extreme heat and changing rainfall patterns. Climate shocks can have major effects on livestock, crops, water supplies and food security.

Piracy and maritime security

Somalia's long coastline became associated internationally with piracy during the 2000s. International naval operations and changes in coastal security significantly reduced attacks, although maritime security remains important.

National identity

The Somali flag's Star of Unity reflects a long-standing idea of Somali national unity. The same symbol appears in wider Somali political and cultural traditions.

Why is Somalia's flag interesting?

Somalia's flag is one of the world's simplest national designs, yet its central star carries a substantial political history. The five-pointed Star of Unity connects the modern state to the broader idea of Somali unity across the Horn of Africa.
