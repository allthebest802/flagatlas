About Palestine

Palestine is a historic and geographic region of the eastern Mediterranean and is also used to describe the State of Palestine, which has observer-state status at the United Nations. The Palestinian territories generally refer to the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip.

The region has been home to many peoples and civilisations and contains places of enormous importance to Judaism, Christianity and Islam.

The flag of Palestine

The Palestinian flag has three horizontal stripes — black, white and green — with a red triangle at the hoist.

The modern design emerged from the Arab Revolt and the Arab nationalist movement.
The same four colours appear in a number of Arab national flags, although their layouts differ.

The flag became a major symbol of Palestinian national identity during the 20th century and is widely used by Palestinian institutions and communities around the world.

Black, white and green

The three horizontal stripes use the traditional Pan-Arab colours. Historical interpretations associate black with the Abbasid period, white with the Umayyad period and green with the Fatimid period.

These associations are part of a wider tradition of using colours to evoke different periods of Arab and Islamic history.

The red triangle

The red triangle at the hoist is another Pan-Arab element and is associated with the Hashemite tradition and the Arab Revolt.

Its position at the hoist gives the flag a strong visual link with other Arab flags using the same colour combination.

The Arab Revolt

The four-colour combination became especially important during the Arab Revolt of 1916–1918 against Ottoman rule. The revolt's flag helped establish the Pan-Arab colour tradition that influenced later national flags.

The Palestinian flag is one of the best-known modern uses of this combination.

A short history of the region

Palestine has an exceptionally long recorded history. Ancient Canaanite societies were followed by Israelite and Judean kingdoms, Assyrian and Babylonian rule, Persian rule, Hellenistic kingdoms, Roman and Byzantine administration, Islamic caliphates, Crusader states, Mamluk rule and the Ottoman Empire.

The modern political history of Palestine was shaped particularly by the end of Ottoman rule, British administration, competing national movements, the creation of Israel in 1948, the 1967 war and the continuing Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

History timeline

c. 10,000 BCE and earlier — Early settlement

The southern Levant contains archaeological evidence of some of the world's earliest settled communities.

Bronze Age — Canaanite cities

Urban centres developed across the region, linked by trade with Egypt, Syria and the wider eastern Mediterranean.

c. 1200–586 BCE — Israelite and Judean kingdoms

The region saw the development of ancient Israelite and Judean states alongside other local peoples and kingdoms.

586 BCE — Babylonian conquest

Jerusalem was conquered and the First Temple was destroyed during the Babylonian conquest of Judah.

539 BCE — Persian rule

The Achaemenid Persian Empire took control of the region after conquering Babylon.

332 BCE — Alexander the Great

Alexander conquered the eastern Mediterranean and the region entered the Hellenistic world.

63 BCE — Roman rule

Roman influence expanded into the region and later became direct Roman administration.

1st century CE — Early Christianity

Christianity emerged in the region, which contains many sites associated with the life of Jesus and the early Christian movement.

7th century — Muslim conquest

Arab Muslim forces conquered the region. Palestine became part of successive Islamic caliphates.

1099 — First Crusade

Crusader forces captured Jerusalem and established Crusader states in the Levant.

1187 — Saladin recaptures Jerusalem

Saladin defeated the Crusaders at the Battle of Hattin and retook Jerusalem.

1291 — Mamluk rule

The Mamluks consolidated control of the Levant after the fall of Acre.

1516 — Ottoman rule begins

The Ottoman Empire defeated the Mamluks and incorporated Palestine into the empire.

1799 — Napoleon's invasion

French forces under Napoleon briefly invaded parts of the region before withdrawing.

Late 19th century — National movements

Modern Palestinian Arab identity developed alongside the growth of Zionism and other national movements in the region.

1916 — Arab Revolt

The Arab Revolt began against Ottoman rule. Its flag popularised the four Pan-Arab colours used in the Palestinian flag.

1917 — Balfour Declaration

The British government expressed support for establishing a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine while also stating that the rights of existing non-Jewish communities should not be prejudiced.

1920–1948 — British Mandate

Britain administered Palestine under a League of Nations Mandate. Arab and Jewish political movements grew increasingly opposed.

1936–1939 — Arab Revolt in Palestine

A major Palestinian Arab uprising opposed British rule and increasing Jewish immigration.

1947 — UN partition proposal

The United Nations proposed partitioning Mandatory Palestine into separate Arab and Jewish states, with Jerusalem under a special international regime.

1948 — Creation of Israel and Palestinian displacement

Israel declared independence and war followed. Hundreds of thousands of Palestinians became refugees in an event Palestinians call the Nakba, meaning catastrophe.

1949 — Armistice lines

Armistice agreements established the boundaries commonly known as the Green Line. The West Bank came under Jordanian control and Gaza under Egyptian administration.

1964 — PLO founded

The Palestine Liberation Organization was established as an umbrella organisation representing Palestinian political and national aspirations.

1967 — Six-Day War

Israel captured the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza, as well as other territories. The occupation became a central issue in the conflict.

1987 — First Intifada

A large Palestinian uprising against Israeli occupation began in the West Bank and Gaza.

1988 — State of Palestine declared

The Palestine National Council proclaimed the State of Palestine, and many countries subsequently recognised it.

1993 — Oslo Accords

Israel and the PLO reached agreements that created the Palestinian Authority and established limited Palestinian self-government in parts of the West Bank and Gaza.

1994 — Palestinian Authority established

The Palestinian Authority began administering areas of the West Bank and Gaza under the Oslo framework.

2000 — Second Intifada

A second major Palestinian uprising began after the collapse of peace negotiations and escalating tensions.

2005 — Israeli withdrawal from Gaza

Israel withdrew its settlements and permanent military presence from inside Gaza while retaining significant control over borders, airspace and access.

2006 — Hamas wins legislative election

Hamas won the Palestinian legislative election, creating a major political split with Fatah.

2007 — Political split between Gaza and West Bank

Hamas took control of Gaza while the Palestinian Authority continued governing parts of the West Bank, producing a lasting Palestinian political division.

2012 — UN non-member observer state

The United Nations General Assembly granted Palestine non-member observer State status.

2014 — Gaza war

A major conflict between Israel and Hamas caused extensive casualties and destruction in Gaza.

2021 — Major escalation

Another severe round of fighting took place between Israel and Hamas, alongside unrest in Jerusalem and elsewhere.

2023 — Hamas attacks and Gaza war

On 7 October 2023, Hamas-led fighters attacked Israel, killing civilians and soldiers and taking hostages. Israel launched a large-scale military campaign in Gaza, resulting in extensive Palestinian civilian deaths, displacement and destruction.

2024 — International legal and diplomatic developments

The war and humanitarian crisis remained central to international diplomacy and legal proceedings, while recognition of Palestine expanded among additional countries.

Today

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict remains unresolved, with questions over statehood, borders, settlements, Jerusalem, refugees, security and Palestinian self-determination at its centre.

Jerusalem

Jerusalem is one of the world's most important religious cities. It is sacred to Judaism, Christianity and Islam and contains sites including the Western Wall, Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Al-Aqsa Mosque compound.

Palestinians claim East Jerusalem as the capital of their future state, while Israel regards Jerusalem as its capital. The city's final status is one of the most sensitive issues in peace negotiations.

The West Bank

The West Bank lies west of the Jordan River and contains major Palestinian cities including Ramallah, Nablus, Bethlehem, Hebron and Jenin.

Its political and administrative geography is complex, with areas under different degrees of Palestinian and Israeli control under the Oslo framework.

The Gaza Strip

The Gaza Strip is a densely populated coastal territory on the Mediterranean Sea. It is home to more than two million Palestinians.

Gaza has experienced repeated conflict, severe humanitarian crises and restrictions on movement and goods. Since the October 2023 Hamas attack, the territory has suffered catastrophic destruction and displacement.

Bethlehem

Bethlehem is famous in Christian tradition as the birthplace of Jesus. The Church of the Nativity is one of Christianity's oldest and most important pilgrimage sites.

The city is in the West Bank and has a predominantly Palestinian Arab population.

Palestinian culture

Palestinian culture includes distinctive traditions of music, poetry, embroidery, food, dance and storytelling.

The traditional embroidered dress known as the thobe is especially important as a marker of regional identity and heritage. Palestinian cuisine includes dishes such as maqluba, musakhan, hummus and falafel.

Palestinian embroidery

Palestinian embroidery, known as tatreez, uses geometric patterns whose styles can identify different regions and communities.

In 2021, Palestinian embroidery was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Palestinian food

Palestinian cuisine draws on the wider Levantine tradition while retaining distinctive regional dishes. Popular foods include musakhan, maqluba, maftoul, hummus, falafel and knafeh.

Olives and olive oil are deeply important to Palestinian agriculture and culture.

The olive tree

The olive tree is one of the strongest symbols of Palestinian life and heritage. Olive cultivation has shaped rural communities and landscapes for centuries.

The annual olive harvest is both an economic activity and an important family and community tradition.

The Palestinian economy

The Palestinian economy includes services, trade, construction, agriculture, tourism and manufacturing.

Economic development is heavily affected by movement restrictions, political uncertainty, access to land and water, the division between Gaza and the West Bank and repeated conflict.

The Dead Sea

The Dead Sea lies at the lowest exposed land elevation on Earth and forms part of the boundary region between the West Bank, Israel and Jordan.

Its exceptionally salty water and mineral-rich mud have made it a major tourism attraction.

The Jordan Valley

The Jordan Valley is an important agricultural area and forms the eastern part of the West Bank. Its warm climate allows the cultivation of dates, vegetables and other crops.

Water resources and control of the valley are important political and economic issues.

Climate

Palestine has a largely Mediterranean climate, with hot, dry summers and cooler, wetter winters. Conditions vary with altitude and proximity to the Mediterranean and Jordan Valley.

Water scarcity is a major environmental and political challenge.

Palestinian national identity

Modern Palestinian national identity developed particularly strongly during the late Ottoman and British Mandate periods and was shaped by the experiences of the 1948 Nakba, 1967 occupation, displacement and the continuing national movement.

The flag, keffiyeh, olive tree and traditional embroidery are among its best-known cultural symbols.

The keffiyeh

The black-and-white keffiyeh has become an internationally recognised symbol associated with Palestinian identity and solidarity.

Its traditional origins are broader than Palestine, but it became particularly associated with Palestinian nationalism during the 20th century.

The Palestinian Authority

The Palestinian Authority was created through the Oslo Accords and administers parts of the West Bank. It is dominated by the Fatah movement.

It does not exercise full sovereign control over the territories and has no governing authority in Gaza, which has been controlled by Hamas since 2007.

Palestine at the United Nations

The State of Palestine has held non-member observer State status at the United Nations since 2012.

More than 140 UN member states recognise Palestine, although recognition is not universal. The question of full UN membership and statehood remains politically contested.

Why is Palestine's flag interesting?

The Palestinian flag is more than a national design: it is one of the strongest symbols of Palestinian national identity and self-determination.

Its four colours connect it to the wider history of Arab nationalism, while its use today reflects the Palestinian national movement and the long-running conflict over land, sovereignty and statehood.
