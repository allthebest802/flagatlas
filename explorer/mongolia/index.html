About Mongolia

Mongolia is a vast landlocked country in East and Central Asia, lying between Russia and China. Much of the country is made up of open grassland steppe, mountains and desert. Ulaanbaatar is the capital and by far the country's largest city.

Mongolia is famous for its nomadic traditions, horses, the Gobi Desert and the extraordinary history of the Mongol Empire founded by Genghis Khan.

The flag of Mongolia

Mongolia's flag has three vertical bands of red, blue and red, with the golden Soyombo symbol on the hoist-side red band.

Blue represents Mongolia's eternal blue sky.
Red represents prosperity and progress. The Soyombo is an ancient national symbol containing imagery associated with fire, sun, moon, earth, water and the traditional Buddhist philosophical heritage of Mongolia.

The modern flag was adopted in 1992 after Mongolia's transition away from the communist system, with the old communist star removed from the Soyombo.

The red bands

The red bands represent prosperity and progress in the modern interpretation of Mongolia's flag.

The colour also creates a strong contrast with the blue central band and the golden Soyombo symbol.

The blue band

The central blue band represents Mongolia's eternal blue sky.

The sky has deep spiritual and cultural importance in Mongolian traditions. Mongolian identity has long been closely connected with the enormous open landscape and the sky above the steppe.

The Soyombo symbol

The Soyombo is one of Mongolia's most important national symbols. It contains several stylised elements arranged vertically.

The upper flame, sun and moon, geometric shapes and yin-yang-like symbol have developed layers of religious, philosophical and national meaning over time.

The Soyombo flame

At the top of the Soyombo is a flame with three tongues. The three flames are commonly associated with prosperity in the past, present and future.

Fire also has an important place in traditional Mongolian culture and symbolism.

The sun and moon

Below the flame are representations of the sun and crescent moon. They are commonly interpreted as symbols of the eternal nature of the Mongolian nation.

The imagery reflects Mongolia's long-standing relationship with the sky and celestial bodies.

The yin-yang symbol

The centre of the Soyombo contains a taijitu-like symbol representing complementary forces.

Its presence reflects the complex religious and philosophical influences that shaped Mongolian visual culture, including Buddhist traditions and earlier Central Asian beliefs.

A short history of Mongolia

Mongolia's history stretches from ancient steppe societies and nomadic confederations to the rise of the Mongol Empire, one of the largest contiguous land empires in history.

After centuries of division and changing imperial influence, Mongolia became closely linked to Qing China before becoming independent in the twentieth century and later establishing a democratic political system.

History timeline

Ancient era — Steppe cultures

Nomadic peoples inhabited and travelled across the Mongolian steppe for thousands of years, developing horse-based pastoral societies.

3rd century BC — Xiongnu Empire

The Xiongnu formed a powerful steppe confederation that became one of the major powers of ancient Inner Asia.

6th–10th centuries — Turkic and other khanates

Successive Turkic and steppe states controlled or influenced parts of the region.

12th century — Temüjin rises

Temüjin united rival Mongol tribes and built the political and military foundation for a new Mongol state.

1206 — Genghis Khan

Temüjin was proclaimed Genghis Khan, or Chinggis Khaan, at a great assembly of Mongol nobles.

13th century — Mongol Empire expands

Mongol armies conquered huge areas of Asia and Europe, creating the largest contiguous land empire in history.

1260s — Yuan dynasty

Kublai Khan established the Yuan dynasty in China, while different branches of the Mongol Empire ruled territories across Eurasia.

14th century — Yuan declines

The Mongol Yuan dynasty in China collapsed in 1368, while other Mongol successor states continued across Inner Asia.

16th century — Buddhism expands

Tibetan Buddhism became increasingly important among Mongolian rulers and communities.

17th century — Qing influence

Much of Mongolia gradually came under the influence and control of the Qing Empire.

1911 — Bogd Khanate

Following the fall of the Qing dynasty, Outer Mongolia declared independence and established the Bogd Khanate.

1921 — People's Revolution

Mongolian revolutionaries, supported by Soviet Russia, defeated opposing forces and established a new political order.

1924 — Mongolian People's Republic

The Mongolian People's Republic was proclaimed, becoming the world's second communist state after Soviet Russia.

1930s — Repression and purges

Religious institutions were heavily targeted and thousands of people were killed during political purges.

1945 — Independence recognised

A referendum confirmed support for Mongolian independence, and the country's status was increasingly recognised internationally.

1961 — United Nations

Mongolia joined the United Nations.

1990 — Democratic Revolution

Mass peaceful demonstrations led to the end of one-party communist rule and the introduction of political and economic reforms.

1992 — New constitution

A democratic constitution established the modern parliamentary republic and the current national flag design.

21st century — Mining and development

Large mineral deposits became increasingly important to Mongolia's economy, particularly coal and copper.

Today

Mongolia is a democratic, landlocked country balancing traditional nomadic culture with rapid urbanisation and a mineral-dependent modern economy.

The Mongol Empire

The Mongol Empire was founded by Genghis Khan in the early thirteenth century and expanded across an enormous area of Eurasia.

At its height it connected China, Central Asia, the Middle East and parts of Eastern Europe. The empire encouraged trade and movement across the vast territory, while its conquests also caused enormous destruction and loss of life.

Genghis Khan

Genghis Khan, born Temüjin, united the Mongol tribes and created a powerful military and political system.

He remains one of the most famous figures in world history and is a central figure in modern Mongolian national identity.

The Mongol military

Mongol armies were famous for their horse archers, mobility, discipline and sophisticated battlefield tactics.

Most soldiers could travel enormous distances on horseback, allowing Mongol forces to move much faster than many of their opponents.

Kublai Khan

Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, established the Yuan dynasty in China and became emperor in 1271.

His reign connected Mongol imperial power with Chinese political and cultural traditions and brought Marco Polo and other foreign travellers into contact with the Mongol court.

Mongolia and the Qing Empire

From the seventeenth century, Mongolia increasingly came under the control of the Qing Empire.

Outer Mongolia remained distinct from China proper, but Qing administration and military influence shaped the region until the dynasty collapsed in 1911.

The Bogd Khanate

After the collapse of the Qing dynasty, Mongolia declared independence in 1911 under the spiritual and political leadership of the Bogd Khan.

The new state sought to preserve Mongolian independence while facing pressure from Russia, China and other regional powers.

The Mongolian People's Republic

In 1924, Mongolia became the Mongolian People's Republic. The country followed a Soviet-aligned communist system for much of the twentieth century.

Religious institutions were suppressed, while Soviet-style education, healthcare and industrial development transformed society.

The Democratic Revolution

In 1990, peaceful demonstrations and political pressure ended one-party communist rule. Mongolia introduced multiparty elections and moved toward a market economy.

The 1992 constitution established the modern democratic republic.

Ulaanbaatar

Ulaanbaatar is Mongolia's capital and largest city. It is home to a large share of the country's population and dominates political, economic and cultural life.

The city's rapid growth has brought major challenges, including traffic, air pollution and pressure on housing and infrastructure.

The Mongolian steppe

The steppe is one of Mongolia's defining landscapes: huge open grasslands stretching across the country.

Pastoral herders have traditionally moved with their livestock between seasonal grazing areas, making the steppe central to Mongolian culture and economy.

The Gobi Desert

The Gobi Desert covers large parts of southern Mongolia and northern China. It contains gravel plains, rocky landscapes, sand dunes and mountains.

The Gobi is also famous for dinosaur fossils and remains one of the world's great desert environments.

The Altai Mountains

The Mongolian Altai forms part of the country's western mountain systems. The region contains high peaks, glaciers, lakes and remote valleys.

It is also home to Kazakh communities famous for traditional eagle hunting.

Mongolia's climate

Mongolia has a strongly continental climate with long, extremely cold winters and short warm summers.

Rainfall is generally low, especially in the south, and the country is exposed to severe winter conditions known as dzud, which can cause mass livestock deaths.

Mongolian wildlife

Mongolia's vast landscapes support wildlife including snow leopards, wolves, saiga antelope, wild Bactrian camels, gazelles and Przewalski's horses.

Large protected areas help conserve species across the steppe and desert.

Przewalski's horse

The Przewalski's horse is one of Mongolia's most famous conservation success stories. The species disappeared from the wild in the twentieth century but has since been reintroduced.

It is considered the only surviving truly wild horse species.

The wild Bactrian camel

The wild Bactrian camel survives in the deserts of Mongolia and China and is one of the world's rarest large mammals.

It is exceptionally adapted to harsh desert conditions and can tolerate extreme temperatures and limited water.

Mongolian culture

Mongolian culture is strongly shaped by nomadic pastoralism, horses, Buddhism, wrestling, music and the vast landscape.

Traditional practices remain important even as more Mongolians live in towns and cities.

The ger

The traditional Mongolian ger, often called a yurt, is a portable round dwelling designed for life on the steppe.

Its structure can be dismantled and moved relatively easily, making it well suited to seasonal pastoral migration.

Naadam Festival

Naadam is Mongolia's most famous national festival. Its traditional competitions are wrestling, horse racing and archery.

The festival is closely associated with Mongolian history, identity and the country's warrior and pastoral traditions.

Mongolian wrestling

Mongolian khüükh wrestling is one of the three traditional Naadam sports. Wrestlers compete in distinctive costumes and use throws, trips and other techniques.

Winning a high-ranking title at Naadam is a major achievement.

Horse culture

Horses are central to Mongolian culture. Children often learn to ride from a young age, and horses remain important for herding, transport, racing and festivals.

Mongolia's relationship with horses is one of the most enduring features of its nomadic heritage.

Mongolian throat singing

Khöömii, or Mongolian throat singing, is a distinctive vocal tradition in which singers produce multiple tones and harmonics.

It is closely associated with the landscape and musical traditions of Inner Asia and has gained international recognition.

Mongolian food

Traditional Mongolian cuisine is strongly influenced by pastoral life and includes mutton, beef, dairy products, flour and noodles.

Popular foods include buuz dumplings, khuushuur and aaruul, a dried curd snack.

Mongolian Buddhism

Tibetan Buddhism became deeply influential in Mongolia from the sixteenth century onward. Monasteries became important religious and cultural centres.

Many monasteries were destroyed or suppressed during the communist period, but Buddhism experienced a major revival after 1990.

Mongolia's economy

Mongolia's modern economy is heavily influenced by mining, particularly coal, copper and gold.

Agriculture and livestock remain important, while tourism and services have grown as the country has opened further to international visitors.

Oyu Tolgoi

Oyu Tolgoi is one of Mongolia's largest mining projects and contains major copper and gold resources.

The mine is economically significant but has also generated debate about foreign investment, environmental impacts and the share of mineral wealth retained by Mongolia.

Livestock and herding

Mongolia has enormous numbers of livestock relative to its population. Herders traditionally keep sheep, goats, cattle, horses and camels.

Cashmere from Mongolian goats is an important export, while herding remains a major source of rural livelihoods.

Mongolia today

Modern Mongolia combines a strong nomadic heritage with a rapidly growing urban population and a resource-based economy.

The country faces challenges including air pollution in Ulaanbaatar, economic dependence on mining, harsh winters, climate change and the pressures of urbanisation.

Why is Mongolia's flag interesting?

Mongolia's flag is unusual because its central emblem is a highly detailed traditional national symbol rather than a simple coat of arms.

The Soyombo connects the modern republic with centuries of Mongolian religious, philosophical and national traditions.
