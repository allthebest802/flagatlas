About Iraq

Iraq is a country in Western Asia at the heart of ancient Mesopotamia. Baghdad is the capital and largest city. Iraq lies between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers and has a history reaching back to some of the world’s earliest cities, writing systems, states and civilisations.

The flag of Iraq

Iraq’s national flag has three horizontal bands of red, white and black. In the centre of the white band is Allahu Akbar, meaning “God is Greatest”, written in green Kufic-style script. The current design was adopted in 2008.

What do the colours mean?

The four colours of the Iraqi flag are part of the Pan-Arab colour tradition. Red is commonly associated with courage and sacrifice, white with peace, black with historic Arab dynasties and green with Islam. Interpretations vary across historical sources.

The green Takbir

The central inscription reads Allahu Akbar, meaning “God is Greatest”. It is written in green and is a prominent Islamic element of the modern flag. The current form replaced an earlier Saddam-era version of the calligraphy.

Kufic script

The inscription uses a stylised Kufic-inspired form of Arabic calligraphy. Kufic is one of the oldest forms of Arabic script and is strongly associated with early Islamic manuscripts and monumental inscriptions.

The Pan-Arab colours

Red, white, black and green are commonly called the Pan-Arab colours. They appear in the flags of several Arab states and connect modern national identity with Arab history, political movements and Islamic heritage.

A short history of Iraq

Iraq is often described as one of the birthplaces of civilisation. Ancient Mesopotamia produced some of the world’s earliest cities, writing systems and states. Later history included Persian empires, the Islamic conquest, the Abbasid Caliphate, Ottoman rule, British influence, the Iraqi monarchy, the Ba’athist period and the wars and political upheavals of the modern era.

Ancient Mesopotamia

Mesopotamia means “land between the rivers” and refers to the region around the Tigris and Euphrates. Much of ancient Mesopotamia lies within modern Iraq. It was a centre of early urbanisation, irrigation, writing and organised government.

The Sumerians

The Sumerians developed some of the world’s earliest cities in southern Mesopotamia. Scribes used cuneiform to record trade, administration, religion and literature.

Cuneiform writing

Cuneiform is one of the earliest known writing systems. It was impressed into clay tablets and used for several languages over thousands of years.

The Epic of Gilgamesh

The Epic of Gilgamesh is one of the world’s oldest surviving works of literature. It follows the legendary king Gilgamesh and his search for meaning and immortality.

The Akkadian Empire

The Akkadian Empire, founded by Sargon of Akkad, united large areas of Mesopotamia and is often regarded as one of the world’s earliest empires.

Babylon

Babylon became one of the ancient world’s most famous cities. Under Nebuchadnezzar II it was a magnificent capital associated with monumental walls, temples and the Ishtar Gate.

Hammurabi’s Code

The Code of Hammurabi is one of the best-known ancient law collections. It records laws concerning property, trade, family matters and punishments and provides valuable evidence about Babylonian society.

The Assyrians

The Assyrian Empire became a major power in the ancient Near East. Cities such as Nineveh and Nimrud contained monumental palaces decorated with detailed stone reliefs.

Nineveh

Nineveh, near modern Mosul, became the capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire. Ashurbanipal’s library preserved thousands of cuneiform tablets, including versions of the Epic of Gilgamesh.

The Persian Empires

Mesopotamia was incorporated into the Achaemenid Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great and later became part of other Persian states, including the Sasanian Empire.

The Islamic conquest

Arab Muslim armies conquered Iraq during the seventh century. Islam transformed the region’s political, religious and cultural life and Iraq became a major centre of Islamic scholarship.

Baghdad and the Abbasid Caliphate

The Abbasid caliph al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762 CE. The city became one of the world’s great centres of learning, trade and culture during the Islamic Golden Age.

The House of Wisdom

The Abbasid-era Bayt al-Hikma, or House of Wisdom, is a famous symbol of Baghdad’s intellectual life. Scholars translated and studied works from Greek, Persian and Indian traditions.

The Mongol sack of Baghdad

In 1258, Mongol forces under Hulagu Khan captured Baghdad. The city was devastated and Abbasid political power there ended, marking a major turning point in Iraqi history.

Ottoman Iraq

From the sixteenth century, much of Iraq became part of the Ottoman Empire. Baghdad, Basra and Mosul developed as important regional centres, although control varied over time.

British influence and independence

During the First World War, British forces occupied Mesopotamia. The modern Iraqi state emerged under a British mandate and became independent in 1932, with King Faisal I as its first king.

The Iraqi Republic

A revolution in 1958 overthrew the monarchy and established a republic. Iraq subsequently experienced political instability, military rule and competing political movements before Ba’athist rule became dominant.

Saddam Hussein

Saddam Hussein ruled Iraq from 1979 until 2003. His government was highly authoritarian and Iraq fought wars with Iran and Kuwait. The 2003 invasion removed his government.

The Iran-Iraq War

The Iran-Iraq War lasted from 1980 to 1988 and caused enormous casualties and destruction. It began after Iraq invaded Iran amid disputes over borders and regional power.

The Gulf War

In 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait. A US-led international coalition expelled Iraqi forces in 1991, followed by severe international sanctions.

The 2003 Iraq War

The US-led invasion of Iraq in 2003 overthrew Saddam Hussein’s government. The following years brought insurgency, sectarian violence and major political change.

Baghdad

Baghdad is Iraq’s capital and largest city, situated on the Tigris. Founded in 762 CE, it became one of the most important intellectual and commercial cities of the medieval world.

Mosul

Mosul lies on the Tigris in northern Iraq, near ancient Nineveh. It has historically been an important trading and cultural centre and suffered severe destruction during the conflict with ISIS.

The Tigris and Euphrates

The Tigris and Euphrates define Iraq’s geography and supported ancient Mesopotamian civilisation. Today the country faces serious water-management challenges involving climate change, upstream dams, salinity and rainfall.

The Mesopotamian Marshes

The Mesopotamian Marshes are distinctive wetlands in southern Iraq. They support fish, birds and traditional communities and have undergone major restoration after extensive twentieth-century drainage.

Climate

Iraq has a predominantly arid and semi-arid climate. Summers can be extremely hot, while northern mountain areas are cooler and wetter. Dust storms and water scarcity are major environmental challenges.

Kurdistan mountains

Northern Iraq contains the Kurdistan mountain ranges, with cooler climates, forests and snow in winter. The region has a distinctive Kurdish cultural identity and includes Erbil and Sulaymaniyah.

Wildlife

Iraq supports gazelles, wild goats, wolves, foxes, otters, wild boar and many migratory birds. Wetlands are particularly important for birdlife.

Iraqi culture

Iraqi culture combines ancient Mesopotamian heritage with Arab, Kurdish, Turkmen, Assyrian, Islamic and other traditions. Music, poetry, calligraphy, architecture and food vary between communities.

Arabic and Kurdish

Arabic and Kurdish are Iraq’s official languages. The country’s linguistic landscape reflects its diverse ethnic and regional communities.

Iraqi food

Iraqi cuisine includes masgouf, dolma, kebabs, kubba, rice dishes, dates and flatbreads. Masgouf, traditionally grilled carp, is particularly associated with Iraqi cuisine.

Dates

Iraq has a long history of date-palm cultivation. The river valleys and southern regions have supported date production for thousands of years and dates remain an important agricultural product.

Oil and the economy

Iraq has some of the world’s largest proven oil reserves, making petroleum central to exports and government revenue. Diversification into services, agriculture, manufacturing and infrastructure remains important.

Erbil Citadel

The Erbil Citadel is a historic fortified settlement in central Erbil. Archaeological evidence suggests occupation over a very long period and the site is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Iraq today

Modern Iraq is a federal parliamentary republic with a young and diverse population, major oil resources and extraordinary archaeological heritage. It continues to rebuild infrastructure and institutions while managing political, economic, environmental and security challenges.

Why is Iraq’s flag interesting?

Iraq’s flag connects a simple Pan-Arab colour scheme with an Islamic inscription. Behind the red, white and black bands is a much deeper story stretching from the world’s earliest cities and writing systems to the modern Middle East.
