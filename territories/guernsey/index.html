About Guernsey

Guernsey is the second-largest of the Channel Islands, lying in the English Channel off the coast of Normandy in France. It gives its name to the Bailiwick of Guernsey, which also includes Alderney, Sark, Herm and other smaller islands. Guernsey is a British Crown Dependency — self-governing, and not part of the United Kingdom or the European Union.

Saint Peter Port is the capital and main harbour. The reigning British monarch is head of state, and the United Kingdom is responsible for the island's defence and foreign affairs. Guernsey issues its own currency, the Guernsey pound, at par with pound sterling.

The flag of Guernsey

Guernsey's flag is a white field with the red cross of Saint George reaching to the edges, and a slimmer gold (yellow) cross placed within it. This gold cross is what sets Guernsey's flag apart from the plain English flag it once shared.

A flag to end the confusion.
Because Guernsey had long used the plain cross of Saint George — the same as England — its flag was easily confused. In 1985 a gold cross was added, drawn from a symbol associated with William the Conqueror, giving the island a distinct flag of its own.

The gold cross is often called the "Cross of Goldan," and links the island's flag to its Norman heritage and the events of 1066.

The gold cross of William the Conqueror

The distinctive gold cross is said to represent a cross reputedly carried on the banner of William, Duke of Normandy — later William the Conqueror — at the Battle of Hastings in 1066. As Guernsey was part of the Duchy of Normandy, the symbol ties the island directly to that history.

When Guernsey decided to update its flag in the 1980s, this gold cross was chosen precisely because it rooted the new design in the island's own Norman past rather than simply copying another flag. The result, adopted in 1985, is one of the more unusual flags in the British Isles.

A short history of Guernsey

Guernsey was part of the Duchy of Normandy, and came under the same ruler as England after Duke William's conquest in 1066. When King John lost mainland Normandy to France in 1204, the Channel Islands remained loyal to the English crown, and have been attached to it ever since as a remnant of the medieval duchy.

The island developed its own parliament and legal system over the centuries. During the Second World War, Guernsey — like the other Channel Islands — was occupied by German forces from 1940 to 1945, the only British soil to be occupied during the war.

Today Guernsey is a prosperous, self-governing Crown Dependency, well known as an international finance centre.

Geography of Guernsey

Guernsey is a small island of roughly 65 square kilometres, with high cliffs along its south coast falling to lower, sandier ground in the north. Its position in the Gulf of Saint-Malo gives it a mild climate and one of the largest tidal ranges anywhere in the world.

Guernsey's neighbours

The Normandy coast of France lies to the east and south, with Jersey — the largest Channel Island — to the south-east. The smaller islands of the Bailiwick, including Alderney, Herm and Sark, lie close by.

People, language and culture

English is the everyday language, while the island's traditional Norman language, Guernésiais, survives among a small number of speakers, and French historically had an official role. The island's Norman heritage is visible in its place-names and customs.

The French writer Victor Hugo spent much of his exile in Saint Peter Port, at Hauteville House, where he completed Les Misérables. Guernsey also gives its name to the Guernsey breed of dairy cattle and to the knitted "guernsey" sweater.

Guernsey today

Guernsey governs itself through the States of Guernsey, its parliament, and manages its own domestic affairs. Financial services form the core of its economy, alongside tourism, horticulture and specialist industries.

As a Crown Dependency, Guernsey sits outside both the United Kingdom and the European Union, while keeping close ties to each.
