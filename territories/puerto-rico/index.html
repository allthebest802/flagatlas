About Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico is a Caribbean island and an unincorporated territory of the United States, lying in the north-eastern Caribbean Sea within the Greater Antilles. It sits east of the Dominican Republic and west of the U.S. and British Virgin Islands, with the Atlantic Ocean to the north and the Caribbean Sea to the south.

San Juan is the capital and largest city. Puerto Ricans have been United States citizens since 1917, and the island has its own constitution and elected governor while remaining under overall U.S. sovereignty. Spanish and English are both official languages.

The flag of Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico's flag has five horizontal stripes — three red and two white — with an equilateral blue triangle at the hoist carrying a single white five-pointed star. It is one of the world's best-known "lone star" flags.

What the parts stand for.
The white star is usually said to represent the island (or commonwealth); the three sides of the triangle, the three branches of republican government. The red stripes are commonly linked to the blood and courage of the people, and the white stripes to liberty and the rights of the individual. As with many flags, these readings are traditional rather than fixed in law.

The shade of blue in the triangle has changed over time. Early versions used a pale sky blue; the blue was later darkened, and since 1995 the official flag has used a dark navy blue similar to that of the United States flag.

The Puerto Rican and Cuban flags

The flag was designed in 1895 in New York by members of the Puerto Rican section of the Cuban Revolutionary Party, who were campaigning for independence from Spain. It was deliberately based on the Cuban flag, with the red and blue swapped: where Cuba's flag has a blue triangle and red stripes, Puerto Rico's has a red-and-white stripe pattern and a blue triangle.

The two designs were meant to express solidarity between the independence movements of the two islands — often described as "two wings of the same bird." The flag was closely associated with the independence cause for decades before becoming an official government symbol.

A short history of Puerto Rico

Before European contact the island was home to the Taíno people, who called it Borikén. Christopher Columbus reached the island in 1493, and it became a Spanish colony that remained under Spanish rule for roughly four centuries, with San Juan growing into a heavily fortified port.

Following the Spanish–American War, Spain ceded Puerto Rico to the United States in 1898 under the Treaty of Paris. Puerto Ricans were granted U.S. citizenship in 1917 through the Jones–Shafroth Act.

In 1952 Puerto Rico adopted its own constitution and became a U.S. commonwealth (in Spanish, Estado Libre Asociado), and the flag was made an official government symbol that same year. Its political status — and the questions of statehood, independence or the current arrangement — remains the subject of ongoing debate, with several referendums held in recent decades.

Geography of Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico consists of one main island together with several smaller islands, including Vieques and Culebra to the east. The main island has a mountainous interior — the Cordillera Central — fringed by coastal plains and beaches, with a tropical rainforest, El Yunque, in the north-east.

Puerto Rico's neighbours

Across the Mona Passage to the west lies the Dominican Republic, while the U.S. and British Virgin Islands lie to the east. The Atlantic Ocean borders the island to the north and the Caribbean Sea to the south.

People, language and culture

Spanish and English are both official languages, with Spanish the everyday language of most Puerto Ricans. The island's culture blends Taíno, Spanish and African influences, seen in its music — such as salsa, bomba and plena — its cuisine and its festivals.

Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens and move freely between the island and the mainland, and there is a large Puerto Rican community across the United States, particularly in the north-east.

Puerto Rico today

Puerto Rico is a self-governing U.S. territory with its own constitution, legislature and elected governor. As a territory rather than a state, it has no voting representation in Congress and its residents do not vote in the general presidential election.

The island's future status is a long-running political question, with support divided between statehood, continued commonwealth status and independence. Several non-binding referendums have been held, but the matter remains unresolved.
