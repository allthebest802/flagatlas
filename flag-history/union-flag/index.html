The Union Flag

How the crosses of England, Scotland and Ireland came together to create one of the world's most recognisable flags.

England and Scotland before union

Before the kingdoms of England and Scotland were politically united, each had its own important flag tradition. England was associated with the red Cross of St George, while Scotland used the white Saltire of St Andrew on blue.

1603 — the crowns are united

When James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, the two kingdoms shared a monarch but remained separate states. A new royal flag was needed to represent the union of the crowns.

The first Union Flag

In 1606, James I ordered a flag combining the crosses of St George and St Andrew. The result placed a white Saltire on blue behind a red St George's Cross edged in white. This was the first Union Flag.

Why is it called the Union Jack?

The terms Union Flag and Union Jack are both widely used. Union Jack originally referred particularly to the flag flown at sea, while Union Flag is the formal term often preferred on land. Today both names are familiar.

1801 — Ireland joins the design

The Act of Union created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland in 1801. The Union Flag was redesigned by incorporating the red Saltire associated with St Patrick. This produced the basic design still used today.

The crosses within the flag

The modern Union Flag combines three traditions: the red Cross of St George for England, the white Saltire of St Andrew for Scotland, and the red Saltire associated with St Patrick for Ireland. Wales is not separately represented because Wales was already incorporated into the Kingdom of England when the original Union was created.

Why the red diagonal cross looks uneven

The red Saltire is deliberately offset relative to the white Saltire. This is sometimes called the counterchanged arrangement. It prevents the flag from giving the impression that the Irish cross simply sits directly on top of the Scottish one.

The Union Flag and empire

The Union Flag became an important symbol of the British state during the growth of the British Empire. It appeared on ships, military colours, official buildings and many colonial flags. Its influence can still be seen in several national and regional flags.

The Union Flag today

The Union Flag remains the national flag of the United Kingdom. It is used by the UK government, armed forces, sporting organisations and citizens, and is one of the most recognisable flag designs in the world.
