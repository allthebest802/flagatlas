St George's Cross

Explore the history of the red cross on a white field, one of England's most recognisable symbols. Its story stretches across medieval Europe, Christianity, warfare and the gradual development of English national identity.

What is St George's Cross?

St George's Cross is a red upright cross on a white field. It is best known today as the flag of England and is one of the principal elements of the Union Flag.

The design is simple, but its history is much more complicated. The cross became associated with England over a long period rather than appearing suddenly as a modern national flag.

It is important to distinguish the cross itself from the later idea of the English national flag. Medieval people did not necessarily understand flags in the same national way that people do today.

FlagAtlas fact: St George's Cross is also used in a number of places and organisations beyond England. The symbol has a wider Christian and European history.

Who was St George?

St George is traditionally remembered as a Christian martyr. Accounts of his life vary and many details are uncertain, but his cult became widespread across Europe during the Middle Ages.

He became particularly associated with the story of St George and the Dragon, in which the saint defeats a dragon threatening a community. The story became one of the most famous legends connected with a Christian saint.

George's popularity spread through churches, military orders, literature and royal patronage. He became a particularly important military saint in medieval Europe.

Was St George's Cross originally English?

No. The red cross on white was not invented specifically for England. Crosses associated with St George were used in a number of European contexts before the design became strongly identified with England.

The cross also appeared in Italian and other European traditions. Genoa, for example, has long used a red cross on white, and the relationship between St George, military symbolism and maritime power helped spread the design.

England's distinctive association with the cross developed gradually through military, religious and royal connections.

St George and medieval warfare

During the Middle Ages, saints could become closely associated with military causes. St George was regarded in parts of Europe as a protector of soldiers and a powerful Christian military symbol.

English armies used the cross in different forms during the medieval period. The symbol could be displayed on shields, banners and clothing as well as on flags.

The use of the cross therefore fitted naturally into the wider medieval tradition of heraldry and military banners.

The Crusades and the cross

St George became strongly associated with crusading traditions, although the history is more complicated than the popular image sometimes suggests.

Different Christian groups used crosses of various colours and forms. The red cross on white eventually became particularly associated with St George, but it should not be assumed that every medieval red cross was automatically an English national symbol.

The Crusades helped increase the visibility and prestige of religious military symbolism across Europe, contributing to the wider environment in which St George's Cross became important.

St George's Cross and England

The association between St George and England strengthened during the later Middle Ages. English rulers, soldiers and institutions increasingly used the saint's imagery.

Edward III established the Order of the Garter in the fourteenth century, placing St George at the centre of one of England's most important chivalric traditions. The saint's chapel at Windsor became the spiritual home of the order.

St George consequently became deeply embedded in English royal and military culture.

When did England adopt St George's Cross?

There was no single moment when the modern English flag was created. The cross appeared in English military and maritime contexts over centuries, while its use as a national symbol developed gradually.

By the late medieval period, St George's Cross was firmly established as a symbol associated with England. It could be used to identify English forces and ships and to distinguish them from other groups.

The design eventually became the simple national flag we recognise today: a red cross on a white field.

St George's Cross at sea

Flags were especially important in medieval and early modern maritime life because ships needed to identify themselves over long distances.

English and later British maritime flags developed from several traditions, including the use of St George's Cross. The cross subsequently became one of the components of the Union Flag.

Maritime use helped reinforce the association between a particular design and the political authority or territory it represented.

The English flag and the Union Flag

St George's Cross is not the same flag as the Union Flag.

The English flag is the red cross on white. The Union Flag combines the crosses associated with England, Scotland and Ireland into a single design.

When the crowns of England and Scotland were united under James VI and I in 1603, the need for a combined royal and maritime flag eventually led to the first Union Flag. The design was changed after the political union with Ireland in 1801.

Remember: England = St George's Cross. The United Kingdom = Union Flag.

Why is the cross red?

The red colour has a long association with Christian martyrdom and the symbolism of St George. However, there is no single universally accepted explanation that accounts for every historical use of the red cross.

Medieval colour symbolism could be religious, heraldic, political or practical. Modern explanations sometimes reduce this complex history to a simple colour code, but the historical evidence is more nuanced.

St George's Cross beyond England

The design is not unique to England. Red crosses on white fields appear in several historical and regional contexts.

The flag of Genoa is one well-known example. The city became an important maritime power, and its red cross became associated with the city and its maritime identity.

St George is also the patron saint of several countries, regions, cities and military organisations. The same symbol can therefore have different meanings depending on its historical context.

St George's Cross today

Today the flag is one of the strongest visual symbols of England. It appears at sporting events, especially football and cricket matches, on public buildings, during national celebrations and in representations of English culture.

It is also used by organisations connected with England's counties, towns and traditions. At the same time, the flag can carry different political or cultural meanings depending on how and where it is displayed.

Its modern popularity is a continuation of a much older history rather than evidence that the symbol was created recently.

A short history timeline

Early Christian tradition

St George becomes a widely venerated Christian martyr and military saint.

Medieval Europe

Crosses associated with St George appear in several European contexts; the symbol is not uniquely English.

Later Middle Ages

St George becomes increasingly important in English military, royal and chivalric traditions.

14th century

The Order of the Garter places St George at the centre of an important English chivalric tradition.

Early modern period

St George's Cross is firmly established in English military and maritime use.

1603 onwards

The English and Scottish crowns are united and the cross becomes one of the elements of the Union Flag.

Today

St George's Cross remains the national flag and a major symbol of England.

Why St George's Cross matters to flag history

St George's Cross is an excellent example of how a modern national flag can develop from several overlapping traditions.

It combines religious symbolism, medieval military culture, royal patronage, heraldry and national identity. None of those elements appeared at exactly the same time.

The result is a flag that looks remarkably simple but contains centuries of history.
