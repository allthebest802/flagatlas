Flag symbolism

The flag is a British Blue Ensign bearing the badge of the British Virgin Islands. The badge features Saint Ursula, the islands' patron saint, with lamps surrounding her. The design connects the territory to Britain while giving it a distinctive local emblem.

About the British Virgin Islands

The British Virgin Islands (BVI) are a group of islands in the Caribbean and a British Overseas Territory. They lie east of Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands and form part of the Virgin Islands archipelago. Tortola is the largest island and the centre of government and commerce.

The territory is known for sailing, beaches, coral reefs and sheltered bays. Road Town, on Tortola, is the capital and the main administrative and commercial centre.

The flag of the British Virgin Islands

The BVI flag uses the British Blue Ensign as its background. In the fly, a white circular badge carries an image of Saint Ursula, associated with the islands, together with lamps around the figure.

Why a Blue Ensign?
The Blue Ensign is used by a number of British Overseas Territories and other organisations with historic links to Britain. The BVI badge makes the flag specifically identifiable as the territory's own flag.

The badge is particularly important to the design because it distinguishes the BVI flag from the plain Blue Ensign and from the flags of other British territories.

Saint Ursula and the eleven lamps

Saint Ursula is the central figure on the BVI badge. Traditional accounts associated with her tell of a large group of companions, and the territory's badge uses lamps as a distinctive visual reference to that story.

A short history of the British Virgin Islands

The islands were inhabited by Indigenous Caribbean peoples before European exploration. European powers later competed for control of the Virgin Islands, and Tortola became associated with English settlement.

Pre-European period

The islands were inhabited by Indigenous peoples before European colonisation.

17th century

English settlement and British control developed on the islands, particularly Tortola.

Modern British territory

The islands developed as a British-administered Caribbean territory and later gained substantial internal self-government.

Today

The BVI remains a British Overseas Territory with its own elected government and a Governor representing the British Crown.

Geography of the British Virgin Islands

The territory includes more than 50 islands and cays, with Tortola, Virgin Gorda, Anegada and Jost Van Dyke among the best known. Tortola is mountainous and contains most of the population, while Anegada is notable for its low, relatively flat landscape and coral-and-limestone character.

Neighbouring islands

The U.S. Virgin Islands lie immediately to the west and south-west. Puerto Rico is farther west, while the Atlantic Ocean and Caribbean Sea surround the wider Virgin Islands region.

People, language and culture

English is the official language. The culture of the BVI reflects African, European and wider Caribbean influences, with music, sailing, food and festivals playing important roles in local life.

The territory has long had close links with the wider Caribbean while retaining its British constitutional connection.

The British Virgin Islands today

The BVI is a self-governing British Overseas Territory. It has an elected House of Assembly and a local government led by a Premier, while the Governor represents the British Crown and retains responsibility for certain areas.

Tourism and financial services are major parts of the economy. The islands are also internationally known for sailing and chartering because of their many sheltered bays and relatively short distances between islands.
