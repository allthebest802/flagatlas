Why Do So Many Flags Use Red, White and Blue?

From France and the United States to Russia, the Netherlands and many others, red, white and blue appear again and again on national flags. But there isn't one single reason.

Published 14 August 2026 · FlagAtlas Guides

There is no single explanation

Red, white and blue became common through several overlapping traditions: European heraldry, historical empires and unions, political movements, maritime flags and ideas that developed around national identity. The same three colours can therefore mean very different things from one country to another.

Why these three colours?

Red, white and blue are visually strong together. They provide clear contrast and can be reproduced relatively easily in cloth and paint. But practical visibility alone does not explain their popularity. Their importance comes from history.

In Europe, these colours appeared in royal arms, city symbols, military banners and maritime traditions long before the modern age of national flags. As states developed more recognisable national identities, existing colours and symbols were often carried into new flags.

France: a revolutionary combination

The French tricolour is one of the world's most influential red, white and blue flags. Its three vertical bands developed during the French Revolution, combining Parisian colours with the white associated with the monarchy. The design became a powerful symbol of the new French nation.

France's tricolour then became an influential example for later flag designers. Its simple three-colour construction was easy to recognise and easy to reproduce.

The Netherlands and the history of red, white and blue

The Dutch flag is another important part of the story. Its horizontal red, white and blue design developed from the earlier Prince's Flag tradition. The colours became strongly associated with the Dutch state and maritime power.

The Dutch tricolour is particularly important because it helped establish the idea that a simple combination of horizontal colour bands could work as a national flag.

The United States: a different story

The American flag also uses red, white and blue, but its symbolism is not simply copied from France or the Netherlands. The colours and stars-and-stripes design developed from the history of the United States and its earlier British connections.

Why do so many European flags use them?

Once red, white and blue were strongly established in European flags, the combination became familiar. Countries did not necessarily choose the colours for the same reason, but the existing tradition made them a natural starting point.

Flags also influence one another. New states often borrow familiar visual ideas while adapting them to their own history and identity.

Does red always mean courage?

No. This is one of the biggest traps when interpreting flags. There are common colour associations, but there is no universal dictionary of flag colours.

Red may represent bravery in one country, revolution in another, a historical kingdom somewhere else, or simply be inherited from an older flag. The meaning belongs to the particular flag and its history.

What about blue and white?

Blue and white are also common because they can represent ideas such as sky, sea, peace or purity — but again, the meaning depends on the country. In some flags, colours were chosen because of dynastic or historical associations rather than a symbolic message.

The bigger lesson

The popularity of red, white and blue shows that flags are part of a visual conversation between countries. Designs are influenced by history, neighbours, empires, revolutions, religion, geography and political movements.

So when you see another red, white and blue flag, don't assume the colours mean the same thing. Look at the story behind that particular flag.
