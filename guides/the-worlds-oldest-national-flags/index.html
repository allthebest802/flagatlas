The World's Oldest National Flags

Flags can survive for centuries, but deciding which is the world's oldest depends on what you mean by 'oldest'. Denmark's Dannebrog is the clearest starting point.

Published 14 August 2026 · FlagAtlas Guides

Denmark's Dannebrog

The Danish flag is widely described as the world's oldest national flag still in continuous use. Denmark's official country website traces its legendary origin to 1219, while noting that the familiar flag became a Danish national symbol later, around the mid-14th century. citeturn1search0turn1search3

What makes a flag “old”?

There is a subtle problem with lists of the world's oldest flags: “oldest” can mean several different things. A design may have appeared centuries ago, but that does not necessarily mean it has been used continuously as a national flag in exactly the same role ever since.

For that reason, the safest way to discuss the oldest flags is to look at documented continuity of use rather than simply finding the oldest-looking flag design in history.

Denmark's Dannebrog

Dannebrog is the Danish flag: a red field carrying a white Scandinavian cross. Denmark's official website calls it the world's oldest national flag and explains that the flag has become deeply embedded in everyday Danish life. citeturn1search3

The famous story says that the flag fell from the sky during the Battle of Lyndanisse in Estonia on 15 June 1219, helping the Danish army to victory. The story is a powerful part of Danish tradition — but Denmark's own official account makes clear that the legendary event should not be treated as established historical fact. citeturn1search0

The legend of 1219

According to the legend, King Valdemar II was leading a crusade in what is now Estonia. During the battle, the Danish army was struggling when a red banner with a white cross supposedly fell from the sky. The Danes rallied, won the battle and adopted the banner.

It is a wonderful flag story, but the story itself was first recorded by historians much later. Denmark's official history of Dannebrog says the legend was first mentioned in the early 16th century and that the flag's use as a Danish national symbol developed later. citeturn1search0

When did it become Denmark's national flag?

Denmark's official account says that red flags with white crosses were used during the medieval crusading period without necessarily being specifically Danish. The design became associated with Denmark around the mid-14th century, making it one of the oldest national flags in continuous use. citeturn1search0

For centuries, Dannebrog was strongly associated with the monarchy. Its use by ordinary people expanded significantly in the 19th century, particularly around the First Schleswig War, when Danish national feeling became much stronger.

A flag used in everyday life

One of the most interesting things about Dannebrog is how often it appears in ordinary Danish life. Denmark's official site describes the flag being used for birthdays, funerals, Christmas decorations, sporting events, gardens and celebrations. citeturn1search0

That makes it more than a state symbol. For many Danes, the flag is part of everyday culture.

Other very old flags

Denmark is the clearest example when discussing a national flag with a long, documented history of continuous use. Other European flags also have medieval or early-modern origins, but comparing them requires care because designs, status and periods of use changed over time.

For example, the flags of Switzerland, Sweden, Austria and the United Kingdom all have long histories, but “oldest” depends on whether you are measuring the age of the symbol, the current design, the state represented by it, or uninterrupted national use.

The key point: Denmark's claim is not simply that a red-and-white cross appeared in 1219. It is that Dannebrog has an exceptionally long history as a national flag, with Denmark describing it as the world's oldest national flag in continuous use.

Why the story still matters

Flags are unusual historical objects because they can survive political changes, wars and changes of government while remaining recognisable. Dannebrog is a particularly strong example: its story mixes medieval legend, royal history, national identity and everyday modern use.

That is one reason flag history is so interesting. A simple design can carry centuries of stories.
